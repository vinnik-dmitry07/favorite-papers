 **Summary:**
The paper investigates the relationship between logical reasoning and natural language by examining brain activity in healthy adults and individuals with severe aphasia. Using functional MRI (fMRI) and behavioral assessments, the study reveals that logical reasoning does not rely on linguistic representations. Instead, it suggests that distinct neural systems support language processing and logical reasoning, with the latter not engaging the language system. The findings challenge the traditional view that language is the primary medium for logical reasoning and provide insights into the neural mechanisms underlying logical reasoning.

**Strengths:**
- The paper addresses a significant and timely question regarding the relationship between language and logic, which is of broad interest to the neuroscience community.
- The study is well-designed, with a clear and well-written manuscript that effectively communicates the findings.
- The use of both healthy adults and individuals with severe aphasia allows for a comprehensive understanding of the role of language in logical reasoning.
- The paper includes a variety of logical reasoning tasks, which are well-chosen and effectively demonstrate the dissociation between language and logic.
- The inclusion of a patient with aphasia who can still perform logical reasoning tasks despite severe language impairments provides compelling evidence for the dissociation between language and logic.
- The study is well-supported by a robust literature review and includes a variety of logical reasoning tasks, which are well-chosen and effectively demonstrate the dissociation between language and logic.

**Weaknesses:**
- The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations, particularly the small sample size of the patient group and the potential impact of this on the generalizability of the findings.
- The fMRI data analysis is limited to a few regions of interest (ROIs), which may not fully capture the neural networks involved in logical reasoning.
- The paper could benefit from a more comprehensive discussion of the implications of the findings for the field of cognitive neuroscience and the broader implications for understanding the nature of logical reasoning.
- The paper could be improved by including more detailed descriptions of the logic tasks used and the specific regions of interest analyzed.
- The manuscript could benefit from a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the study, including the small sample size and the potential impact of this on the generalizability of the findings.

**Questions:**
- Could the authors clarify whether the patients were given any feedback on their performance during the induction task?
- How were the rules for the induction task chosen, and were they randomly selected or based on some criteria?
- How do the authors interpret the finding that the language network is not engaged during logical reasoning, and what are the implications of this finding for the nature of logical reasoning?
- Could the authors discuss the implications of their findings for the broader field of cognitive neuroscience, particularly in terms of how these findings might influence our understanding of the nature of logical reasoning?
- How do the authors respond to the suggestion that the dissociation between language and logic might be due to the complexity of the logic tasks used?
- Could the authors provide more details on the logic tasks used, including the specific rules and the number of problems presented?
- How do the authors address the potential confounding factors, such as the influence of education level on the performance in the logic tasks?
