 **Summary:**
The paper introduces rStar-Math, a method that utilizes Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to enhance the math reasoning capabilities of small language models (SLMs) without relying on distillation from superior models. The approach involves a novel code-augmented CoT data synthesis method, which generates step-by-step verified reasoning trajectories using MCTS, and a process reward model training method that avoids naive step-level score annotation. The authors also propose a self-evolution recipe to iteratively improve the reasoning capabilities of the SLMs. The methodology is evaluated on various math reasoning tasks, showing that rStar-Math can surpass the performance of OpenAI o1 on certain benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness and potential for broader applications in math reasoning.

**Strengths:**
- The paper introduces a novel approach to training small language models (SLMs) for math reasoning, which does not rely on distillation from superior models, instead using a self-evolution recipe and a process reward model training method.
- The methodology is well-explained, with clear figures and detailed explanations that make it easy to follow.
- The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method through extensive experiments, showing that rStar-Math can surpass the performance of OpenAI o1 on certain benchmarks.
- The paper is well-written, with a clear and concise presentation of the methodology and results.
- The use of Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to generate training data for math reasoning is a novel and interesting approach.

**Weaknesses:**
- The paper lacks a detailed comparison with other state-of-the-art methods, particularly in terms of computational resources and training data requirements.
- There is a lack of discussion on the limitations of the proposed method, such as the potential for overfitting and the scalability of the approach to other domains.
- The paper does not sufficiently address the generalizability of the method to other domains beyond math reasoning.
- The evaluation of the method is limited to specific benchmarks, and there is a need for more comprehensive testing across different types of math problems.
- The paper does not adequately discuss the potential negative societal impacts of the proposed method.
- The method's reliance on MCTS for generating training data may limit its applicability to other types of reasoning tasks.

**Questions:**
- Can the authors provide a more detailed comparison with other state-of-the-art methods in terms of computational resources, training data requirements, and performance metrics?
- How does the proposed method perform on other types of math problems, such as geometry-based problems?
- Could the authors discuss the potential negative societal impacts of the proposed method, particularly in terms of its application to other domains beyond math reasoning?
- How does the method handle the potential for overfitting, especially given the iterative training process?
- Can the authors provide more details on the training data used for the policy and reward models, particularly in terms of its diversity and quality?
- How does the method compare to other methods that use different types of training data, such as synthetic data or data generated by other methods?
