## Summary:

This paper introduces a compelling investigation into the reliability of Activation Oracles (AOs), a relatively recent interpretability technique that leverages language models to decode the internal activations of neural networks. The core contribution lies in demonstrating a counterintuitive phenomenon: fine-tuning AOs on activations from models that persistently represent a hidden concept can lead to the emergence of concept-specific blind spots. Instead of becoming more adept at identifying the hidden concept, the fine-tuned AOs become increasingly unable to recover it, a phenomenon the authors term 'concept-specific anti-reading.' This finding challenges the assumption that learned interpretability tools simply expose pre-existing information within a model; rather, they can actively shape the accessibility of that information. The authors employ a controlled 'Taboo Word Guessing' task, where subject models are fine-tuned to internally use a hidden target word while avoiding its direct disclosure. AOs are then trained on these subject models' activations and evaluated on their ability to recover the hidden concept. The key empirical findings reveal that fine-tuned AOs exhibit a significant degradation in their ability to recover the concept that was present during their training, despite the fact that the concept remains decodable from the subject model's activations and is recoverable by a base AO. This suggests that the failure is not due to the absence of the concept in the model's representations but rather a failure in the AO's readout pathway. The authors further explore this phenomenon through mechanistic analyses, including linear probes, LogitLens readouts, and layer-range ablations, which localize the effect to FT-AO-specific updates around the mid-to-late readout transition. The paper's significance lies in its cautionary message for the field of interpretability: learned interfaces can develop biases and blind spots that are not simply reflections of the underlying model's representations. This work underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of how interpretability tools themselves influence what information is accessible and what remains hidden.
