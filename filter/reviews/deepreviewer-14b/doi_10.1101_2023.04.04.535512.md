## Summary:

This paper investigates the emergence of belief-like representations in recurrent neural networks (RNNs) trained through reinforcement learning (RL). The central hypothesis is that RNNs, when trained to estimate value in partially observable environments, can implicitly learn representations that encode information about the underlying hidden states, akin to Bayesian beliefs. The authors contrast this approach with a belief-based model that explicitly computes belief states using a Bayesian update and then learns a value function. They employ two experimental paradigms previously used in neuroscience to study belief-like computations in mice, involving probabilistic reward delivery and temporal discounting. The core finding is that RNNs, specifically Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs), trained with temporal difference (TD) learning can generate reward prediction errors (RPEs) that closely match those of the belief-based model and, crucially, align with experimental recordings of dopamine neuron activity in mice. The authors demonstrate this alignment through various analyses, including linear decoding of hidden states from RNN activity, comparison of dynamical properties, and examination of the RNN's fixed points. They further show that the capacity of the RNN, controlled by the number of hidden units, influences the degree to which its representations resemble beliefs. The paper argues that this implicit learning of belief-like representations by RNNs offers a plausible mechanism for how animals can perform Bayesian computations without explicitly maintaining belief states, which can be computationally demanding. This work bridges the gap between computational neuroscience and machine learning by using deep RL techniques to model animal behavior and by providing a mechanistic explanation for how belief-like representations might emerge from neural activity. The study's significance lies in its demonstration that complex, belief-like representations can arise from simple RL training objectives, suggesting that animals might not need to explicitly compute beliefs to behave optimally in partially observable environments. This work also provides a valuable tool for neuroscientists by offering a way to interpret neural activity in terms of underlying computational states, even when those states are not explicitly represented in the neural code.
