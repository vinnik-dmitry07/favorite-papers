## Summary:

This paper presents a critical re-evaluation of the information bottleneck (IB) theory as applied to deep learning, specifically challenging the claims made by Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby in their 2017 work. The authors meticulously investigate the hypothesis that deep neural networks undergo two distinct phases during training—an initial fitting phase followed by a compression phase—and that this compression phase is causally linked to good generalization performance. Furthermore, they question the assertion that this compression is a consequence of the diffusion-like behavior of stochastic gradient descent (SGD). Through a combination of analytical arguments and empirical studies, the authors demonstrate that the observed compression in Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby's work is not a general phenomenon but rather an artifact of the specific activation function used (tanh) and the method employed to estimate mutual information (binning of continuous-valued hidden layer activities). The authors also challenge the causal link between compression and generalization, showing that networks can generalize well without undergoing a compression phase. They further investigate the role of task-irrelevant information, finding that while networks may compress this information, it is not a necessary condition for good generalization. Overall, this work provides a valuable corrective to the field, highlighting the importance of carefully examining the assumptions and methodologies used in information-theoretic analyses of deep learning. The authors' findings suggest that the IB theory, as presented by Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby, may not be a general explanation for the success of deep learning, and that the relationship between information dynamics and generalization is more complex than previously thought.
