## Summary:

This paper delves into the intriguing question of whether activation oracles (AOs), when trained on the internal activations of fine-tuned language model subjects, become proficient readers of the concepts those subjects are designed to hide. The authors introduce a 'Taboo Word Guessing' setting, inspired by Cywinski et al. (2025), as a controlled environment to investigate this issue. In this setup, a language model subject is fine-tuned to internally represent and utilize a hidden concept while avoiding to explicitly reveal it. The authors then train AOs on these fine-tuned subjects and compare their performance to that of a base AO trained on a standard subject and a cross-FOA trained on subjects with different hidden concepts. The core contribution of this work lies in its exploration of the differences between behavioral secrecy and concept-specific anti-reading. The authors demonstrate that while the AOs do exhibit some degree of decodability for the hidden concepts, their ability to verbalize the target concept is significantly lower than that of the base AOs, especially when the AO is trained on a subject with a different hidden concept. This finding suggests that fine-tuning the AO on the subject data does not necessarily lead to improved reading ability but rather results in the AO becoming a concept-specific anti-reader, selectively failing to recover the concept that was persistently present during its own training. The authors further investigate the mechanisms behind this phenomenon through linear probes, LogitLens analysis, and layer-range ablations, providing evidence that the target concept remains decodable within the AO's representation, but its verbalization is suppressed in the AO's readout. The paper's findings have significant implications for the reliability of AOs as interpretability tools, highlighting that AOs, like any other learned model, can develop blind spots and selectively omit information present in the subject's internal representations. This work underscores the need for caution when using AOs to audit model internals, as they may not always accurately reflect the information present in the subject model. The paper also contributes to our understanding of how fine-tuning affects the internal representations and readout mechanisms of AOs, revealing that simply exposing the AO to the subject's activations is not sufficient for it to become a reliable reader of the subject's internal states. The authors' work is a valuable contribution to the field of model interpretability, prompting a re-evaluation of the assumptions underlying the use of activation oracles as readout mechanisms for understanding internal model states.
