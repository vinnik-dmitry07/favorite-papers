## Summary:

This paper introduces AHE (Agentic Harness Engineering), a novel framework for iteratively improving coding agent harnesses, which are the environments that enable and constrain the actions of large language models (LLMs) in software engineering tasks. The core idea behind AHE is to treat the harness as an external, modifiable system that can be optimized through a closed-loop process. This process involves three key components: component observability, experience observability, and decision observability. Component observability involves decoupling the harness into independent, file-level components (such as the system prompt, tools, middleware, and long-term memory) that can be edited without affecting others. Experience observability leverages the Agent Debugger to generate a layered evidence corpus from agent trajectories, providing structured data for tracking the consequences of harness changes. Finally, decision observability pairs each edit with a self-declared prediction about its impact on future performance, allowing the agent to evaluate the effectiveness of each change. The AHE framework operates by first seeding the harness with a minimal configuration, then iteratively applying changes based on the evidence corpus and the evolving agent's predictions. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of AHE on the Terminal-Bench 2 dataset, achieving a significant improvement in pass@1 from 69.7% to 77.0% after ten iterations. Furthermore, they show that the optimized harness, when transferred to different base models and task surfaces, maintains a high success rate, indicating the generalizability of the learned components. The paper also includes ablation studies that highlight the importance of each component in the AHE framework, showing that the system prompt alone can regress, while other components contribute more additively to the overall performance. The authors emphasize that AHE is a controlled research prototype, and the results should be interpreted with caution due to the high variance and the specific experimental setup. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to harness optimization, highlighting the potential of structured, evidence-driven methods for improving the performance of coding agents.
