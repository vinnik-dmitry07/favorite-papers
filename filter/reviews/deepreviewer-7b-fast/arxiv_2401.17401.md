## Summary:

This paper investigates the limitations of step-size normalization methods, such as RMSProp and Adam, in the context of continual learning, where the goal is to adapt to non-stationary environments. The authors argue that these methods, which normalize gradients to adapt step sizes, can be suboptimal for tracking time-varying optimal parameters. They contrast this with step-size optimization methods, specifically the Incremental-Delta-Bar-Delta (IDBD) algorithm, which directly optimizes step sizes with respect to the overall objective function. Through analytical and empirical analysis, the paper demonstrates that IDBD can more effectively adapt to changing optimal step sizes, particularly in scenarios where some parameters require constant updates while others require more frequent adjustments. The core argument is that normalization, while beneficial in stationary settings, hinders the ability to make precise, non-proportional updates necessary for tracking. The authors present experiments on simplified linear regression problems, including a 'weight-flipping' task and a '1D noisy rate-tracking' problem, to illustrate the limitations of step-size normalization and the advantages of step-size optimization. The experiments show that RMSProp and Adam often fail to converge to the optimal step size trajectory, while IDBD consistently outperforms these methods. The paper concludes by suggesting that a hybrid approach, combining the stability of normalization with the adaptability of optimization, could be a promising direction for future research in continual learning. The work highlights the importance of step-size adaptation in non-stationary environments and provides a compelling case for exploring alternative optimization methods beyond standard normalization techniques. However, the paper's analysis is limited by its focus on simple linear regression tasks and a lack of comparison with other advanced optimization algorithms, which raises questions about the generalizability of the findings to more complex, non-linear settings and real-world applications.
