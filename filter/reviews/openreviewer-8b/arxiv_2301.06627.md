# Review

## Summary
This paper introduces a distinction between formal and functional linguistic competence in LLMs, drawing inspiration from cognitive neuroscience. It argues that while LLMs excel in formal linguistic competence, they are still lacking in functional linguistic competence. The authors posit that achieving human-like language capabilities may require models to develop mechanisms specific to each type of competence.

## Strengths
1. The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
2. The authors provide a clear and compelling motivation for the formal-functional competence distinction, grounded in cognitive neuroscience.
3. The paper offers a comprehensive and insightful analysis of LLMs' performance on formal and functional linguistic tasks.
4. The discussion on the limitations of current LLMs in functional linguistic competence, despite their proficiency in formal competence, is both relevant and timely.

## Weaknesses
1. The paper could benefit from a more explicit discussion of the implications of the proposed framework for the development of future LLMs and natural language processing systems.
2. While the paper focuses on the distinction between formal and functional linguistic competence, it would be valuable to explore the relationship between these two types of competence in greater depth.

## Questions
1. What are the potential implications of the formal-functional competence framework for the design and evaluation of future LLMs and natural language processing systems?
2. How might the proposed framework influence the development of new algorithms, architectures, or training techniques for LLMs?
3. Can you provide more examples of the implications of the formal-functional competence framework for real-world natural language processing applications?
