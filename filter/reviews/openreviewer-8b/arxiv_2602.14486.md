# Review

## Summary
The paper identifies two confounding factors (width and depth) that can lead to incorrect conclusions when measuring the similarity of representations learned by neural networks. The authors propose a permutation-based method to calibrate similarity scores, which can be used to test the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis (which states that representations in neural networks converge to shared local neighborhood relationships).

## Strengths
- The paper is well-written and easy to follow.
- The authors identify two important confounding factors that can lead to incorrect conclusions when measuring the similarity of representations learned by neural networks.
- The authors provide a permutation-based method to calibrate similarity scores, which can be used to test the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis (which states that representations in neural networks converge to shared local neighborhood relationships).

## Weaknesses
- The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, such as the assumption of exchangeability.
- The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the implications of the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis, such as what are the consequences of this hypothesis for our understanding of neural networks and representation learning.

## Questions
- How sensitive is the proposed method to the choice of the significance level α?
- How does the proposed method perform on representations that are not trained on large-scale datasets? What are the implications of this for the generalizability of the method?
- What are the consequences of the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis for our understanding of neural networks and representation learning?
- How does the Aristotelian Representation Hypothesis relate to other hypotheses or theories about representation learning, such as the Information Bottleneck theory or the Predictive Coding framework?
