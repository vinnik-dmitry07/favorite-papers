# Review

## Summary
This paper investigates the relationship between language and logical reasoning in the human brain. The authors use fMRI to scan healthy adults while they performed inductive and deductive reasoning tasks, and also tested individuals with severe aphasia, who have significant language impairments due to brain damage. The key finding is that language areas in the brain are not involved in logical reasoning. Instead, inductive reasoning engages the multiple demand network, while deductive reasoning recruits a distinct set of frontal and parietal areas.

## Strengths
The paper is well-written and easy to follow. The study is well-motivated and the findings are clearly presented. The use of both fMRI in healthy adults and behavioral tests on aphasic patients provides a strong methodological approach. The results are clearly presented and the discussion is thorough. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution to understanding the neural basis of reasoning.

## Weaknesses
The study is limited to native English speakers, and only two aphasic participants were tested, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. The tasks used in the study are also limited to simple inductive and deductive reasoning, and more complex reasoning tasks may involve different cognitive processes. The paper could benefit from testing the participants with more complex logical reasoning tasks.

## Questions
1. How do the authors think their findings might generalize to other languages? Is language of thought a property of natural language or a property of thought/conceptualization itself? 
2. The authors mention that natural language is riddled with vagueness, ambiguity, etc. and therefore may not be a reliable medium for inference. But isn't this also true for the artificial language of mathematics? Don't mathematicians also rely on context, interpretation, etc. to understand mathematical statements? 
3. The authors mention that future work could test whether logical reasoning depends on visuo-spatial, abstract, or domain-specific representations. What kind of representations do the authors think are most likely to be the language of thought?
