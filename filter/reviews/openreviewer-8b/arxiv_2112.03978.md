# Review

## Summary
The authors review the role of attractor dynamics in the brain. They describe the different types of attractors that can arise in recurrent neural networks and the mechanisms that lead to their emergence. They then discuss the possible roles these attractors can play in the brain, such as robust representation, memory, sequence generation, integration, and decision making. They then discuss the evidence for attractor dynamics in the brain, describing the criteria for establishing the presence of attractors and how these can be tested experimentally. They then review a number of examples of brain circuits in which attractor dynamics have been identified, including discrete attractors in the form of bistability, multistability, continuous attractors in the form of integrators (e.g., eye movements), and oscillators (e.g., circadian rhythms). They conclude by discussing cases in which the evidence for attractor dynamics is less clear cut.

## Strengths
The paper is well written and clear. It provides a good overview of the different types of attractors that can arise in recurrent neural networks and the mechanisms that lead to their emergence. It also provides a good overview of the different roles that these attractors can play. The discussion of the evidence for attractor dynamics in the brain is particularly thorough. The authors distinguish between necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of attractors and discuss the evidence for and against the presence of attractors in a number of different brain circuits.

## Weaknesses
The paper is not particularly novel. It does not present any new theory or experimental results. Its main contribution is to provide a review of the literature on attractor dynamics in the brain. This in itself is a valuable contribution, but it is not sufficient for a top-tier conference like ICLR. I think the paper would be more suitable for a review or commentary in a neuroscience journal.

## Questions
- In the discussion of continuous attractors, the authors mention that "the existence of stationary continuous attractors is fragile in the sense that it depends on the maintenance of continuous symmetries". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What kind of symmetries are required, and how are they related to the mechanisms that lead to the emergence of continuous attractors?

- In the discussion of continuous attractors, the authors mention that "the strength of network feedback must be ﬁnely tuned". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the consequences of this fine-tuning, and how is it related to the robustness of the attractor dynamics?

- In the discussion of continuous attractors, the authors mention that "all states on the attractor manifold are neutrally stable so movements along the attractor are allowed". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the consequences of this, and how does it affect the functioning of the network?

- In the discussion of discrete attractors, the authors mention that "the attractor states in Hopﬁeld-like networks typically have highly mixed and overlapping neural memberships". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the consequences of this, and how does it affect the functioning of the network?

- In the discussion of discrete attractors, the authors mention that "in a special case of Hopﬁeld networks, neurons are partitioned into largely disjoint groups with self-excitation within groups and inhibition between groups". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the consequences of this, and how does it affect the functioning of the network?

- In the discussion of discrete attractors, the authors mention that "the attractors of simple rate-based networks without synaptic delays and with arbitrary symmetric weight matrices consist entirely of ﬁxed points". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the consequences of this, and how does it affect the functioning of the network?

- In the discussion of discrete attractors, the authors mention that "some non-symmetric networks can also support point attractors, but this is not the generic case and can require additional mechanisms like homeostatic plasticity". Can the authors elaborate on this point? What are the additional mechanisms that are required, and how do they affect the functioning of the network?
