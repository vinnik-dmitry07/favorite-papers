Structured Scaling of AI Discovery Across Diverse Scientific Domains

Wizard Intelligence Learning Lab, Stanford University Peking University, Tsinghua University The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)

Homepage

Abstract

Scientific discovery often requires many cycles of proposing, testing, and refining candidate solutions. Language models can increasingly participate in these loops, but simply generating more attempts does not ensure progress: parallel searches may duplicate one another and iterative refinement may become trapped in poor directions. The central challenge is therefore not only to scale AI-driven discovery, but to structure that scaling so that evaluation signals compound over time. Here we introduce SimpleTES ( Simple T est-time E valuation-driven S caling), a framework that focuses on the structured scaling of AI discovery loops, organizing evaluator queries across independent trajectories, iterative refinement, local candidate selection, and the selective reuse of evaluated histories. Drawing on structural features of scientific communities, SimpleTES uses a single open-source GPT-OSS model to establish new state-of-the-art solutions across 28 open-ended problems in diverse scientific domains ranging from quantum physics and astronomy to biology, AI, and mathematics. These include a 24.5% reduction in quantum circuit compilation overhead, up to 23% lower propulsive cost for deep-space trajectories, a 2.17 × 2.17\times faster lasso-path solver, an 8.5% lower-error whole-brain neural-activity predictor, the fastest reported TriMul kernel, and new mathematical constructions beyond prior human or AI records. We further post-train the model for long-horizon discovery by assigning each attempt the final outcome of the trajectory it helped produce. This improves performance on both training and held-out mathematics problems, further advancing the frontier. Together, these results establish structured scaling as a general mechanism for advancing AI scientific discovery.

### Introduction

Over the past decade, the role of AI in scientific discovery has changed significantly. Early successes in AI for science came largely from specialized models that served as predictors or optimizers for well-defined scientific objects, such as protein structures, quantum wavefunctions, or molecular potentials [ 65 , 21 , 107 , 151 , 169 ] . More recently, language models have been treated as researchers, generating candidate solutions, writing and revising the programs that implement them, and carrying a line of inquiry across many steps in response to evaluator feedback [ 17 , 48 , 85 , 100 ] . For instance, recent systems build the loop around evolutionary search procedures, as exemplified by AlphaEvolve and related approaches [ 100 , 114 , 6 , 73 ] , or around tree search [ 9 ] , while other works adapt the model through test-time reinforcement learning on a single problem [ 155 , 165 ] . Despite differences in domains, models, and search heuristics, these systems share a common building block: the propose-evaluate-refine discovery loop that drives new discovery results [ 114 , 43 , 123 , 165 ] .

More broadly, the human research community advances science collectively by scaling this loop in a complex, decentralized manner: researchers around the world pursue candidate solutions in parallel, driven by different inspirations, test them against the current baselines, and use the resulting feedback to shape the next attempts [ 74 ] . With LLMs taking the role of individual researchers in the loop, the entire scaling can be designed completely differently: an organized, structured way to drive scientific breakthroughs more efficiently and effectively. We name this new scaling dimension evaluation-driven scaling , because it is primarily driven by evaluation signals from empirical measurements, simulations, formal checks, or performance profiles, which later on become inspirations of next proposals. Different from standard model scaling that scales up parameters and training data [ 67 , 56 ] , or classical test-time scaling that scales through longer reasoning traces or additional agent turns [ 129 , 158 , 96 , 174 , 83 , 69 ] , evaluation-driven scaling targets challenging, open-ended problems that fundamentally require explorations, and a larger number of loops does not automatically imply better results. While showing promise in making discoveries, existing systems leave unexamined how the loop itself should be organized in a structured way. This leads to a central question: How can AI-driven discovery loops be scaled in a structured way to accelerate scientific breakthroughs?

This paper introduces SimpleTES ( Simple T est-time E valuation-driven S caling), a framework for scaling AI-driven discovery by structuring how evaluator queries are allocated and how evaluated histories are grown, filtered, and reused. The central challenge is to determine how early attempts and their evaluation signals should guide later proposals, and which search structures allow progress to compound on open-ended problems. SimpleTES draws on structural features of scientific communities: locally, individual researchers test a few variants and carry forward the best; over time, researchers selectively learn from prior results based on relevance, performance, and reputation; collectively, researchers build on one another’s ideas to refine and propose new solutions; and in parallel, multiple lines of inquiry develop and compete. SimpleTES operationalizes these features through a compact and structured design tuple ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) . Global width C C maintains independent trajectories, refinement depth L L lets feedback accumulate within each trajectory, local sample size K K filters candidate variants before they shape future refinement, and the proposal constructor Φ \Phi selects and compresses useful prior solutions, failures, and feedback into the next prompt. This reframes discovery-loop scaling as a problem of managing evaluated histories, so that additional evaluations compound into stronger trajectories rather than merely producing more attempts. The key distinction between SimpleTES and existing AI discovery systems such as AlphaEvolve lies in the focus. Existing systems typically seek to maximize discovery performance through customized agentic search procedures. SimpleTES instead asks how the discovery loop itself should be organized and scaled. By isolating this structure, SimpleTES becomes a simple, structured, and highly parallelizable system that scales efficiently to tens of thousands of evaluator queries, yielding better results using smaller open-source models compared to prior systems that used larger proprietary LLMs. A comprehensive comparison can be found in Supplementary B.1 .

We apply SimpleTES on 28 open-ended problems across diverse scientific domains, spanning quantum circuit compilations, astrodynamics trajectory designs, GPU kernel implementation, LLM scaling law designs, biological and time-series algorithms optimization, and mathematical constructions. On all problems, SimpleTES discovers the state-of-the-art solutions using a single open-source GPT-OSS model, surpasses both human experts and other AI discovery systems that use an order of magnitude larger proprietary models. To illustrate, SimpleTES improves the best-known bound for the Erdős minimum-overlap problem, open in extremal combinatorics since 1955, ahead of every prior human and machine record [ 100 ] . It discovers quantum compilation policies that reduce expensive qubit-swap gates by over 20% relative to the widely used LightSABRE baseline [ 176 ] . It improves the fuel efficiency of Solar System exploration mission trajectories, reducing propulsive costs by up to 23% relative to references constructed from historical encounter sequences under the same orbital-dynamics evaluator [ 148 , 121 ] . It implements the fastest TriMul kernel, the triangle multiplicative update central to AlphaFold3 [ 66 , 1 ] , and a lasso path solver 2.17 × 2.17\times faster than the widely used glmnet [ 39 ] . In neuroscience, it also discovers a state-of-the-art whole-brain neural-activity predictor, reducing error by 8.5 % 8.5\% on the long-horizon time-series forecasting task in ZAPBench [ 88 ] relative to the proprietary-model-driven algorithm discovery result [ 9 ] . More than any single record, what matters is that one unchanged procedure produces all of them, from abstract mathematical constructions to executable scientific programs, showing that SimpleTES can serve as a general mechanism for discovery.

The above results come from the fixed, off-the-shelf GPT-OSS model, which is not specifically optimized for the long-horizon discovery that SimpleTES performs: standard post-training tends to reward outputs that score well immediately, whereas discovery often turns on unconventional attempts that score poorly on their own yet lead to much stronger solutions. This raises a natural question: how can the LLM itself be deliberately trained for long-horizon discovery? We address this by post-training the model on its own successful trajectories, crediting each attempt by the final outcome of its trajectory rather than its immediate score. The improved model then drives the same search, now less myopic and more willing to build on early failures in pursuit of a stronger eventual solution. We study training primarily in mathematics, across a range of related open-ended problems. Post-training improves the entire outcome distribution, raising both the average score across independent trajectories and the best score on problems where the off-the-shelf GPT-OSS model scores poorly, and it transfers to held-out mathematical problems. These results suggest the trained model learns to search for strong eventual solutions rather than optimizing for immediate scores.

In summary, SimpleTES takes the scaling of the loop as its object of study, and asks how a fixed budget of evaluator queries should be structured. By formalizing this structured scaling with the design tuple ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) , SimpleTES shows superior performance using the open-source model, surpassing existing systems that uses larger proprietary models. SimpleTES uses a single configuration to handle all five domains and discover 28 SOTA results, with only the evaluator changing between problems. ### Overview of SimpleTES

SimpleTES is a test-time framework for scientific discovery on problems that have a verifiable evaluation protocol , which we call an evaluator . An evaluator is the system’s interface to the scientific objective: given a candidate solution, it returns a numerical score together with auxiliary feedback such as verifier messages, error traces, or runtime statistics. Depending on the task, it may compute the objective exactly (an exact verifier), estimate it empirically (a measurement), or approximate it (a heuristic proxy). The objective is to find the highest-scoring candidate under the evaluator.

Formally, a problem is specified by an instruction x 0 x_{0} , and the goal is a solution y y that maximizes the score r r output by an evaluator V V . To search for such a solution, an LLM G G plays the role of a researcher, drawing candidates y ∼ G ⁡ ( x ) y\sim G(x) from a proposal x x built from the instruction x 0 x_{0} together with the accumulated history of previously evaluated solutions, their scores, and the evaluator’s feedback. SimpleTES runs this as a propose–evaluate–refine loop: G G proposes a candidate, V V evaluates it, and its score and feedback are folded into the history that shapes the next proposal.

SimpleTES organizes this loop through a compact design tuple ( C , L , K , Φ ) , \displaystyle(C,L,K,\Phi), (1) where C C is the number of parallel trajectories, L L the number of refinement steps per trajectory, K K the number of candidates drawn per step, and Φ \Phi the proposal constructor that assembles each new proposal from a trajectory’s history. SimpleTES launches C C independent trajectories and advances each for L L refinement steps. At each step, Φ \Phi builds a proposal x x from the history so far, G G draws K K candidate solutions from x x , V V scores them, and the highest-scoring one is appended to the trajectory. Once every trajectory has completed, SimpleTES returns the best solution across all of them, having spent N = C × L × K N=C\times L\times K evaluator queries.

This decomposition is what gives SimpleTES its structure: each of the three dimensions targets a distinct, independent way an undirected search fails, so together they form a small and non-redundant set of controls on the search, which we analyse and ablate in the Methods. Refinement depth L L lets feedback accumulate, so later attempts build on earlier ones, but a single deep trajectory is not enough, since once early attempts bias the search toward a narrow direction, further refinement tends to reinforce that basin rather than escape it. Global width C C counters this by running independent trajectories in parallel, so competing directions mature without influencing one another early on. Local sample size K K addresses noise in the LLM’s output: drawing several candidates at each step and committing only the highest-scoring one keeps a weak or non-executable candidate from corrupting every later refinement built on it. Because the three act on different failure modes, they can be adjusted independently, and the budget N N has a clear meaning as the way that scaling is allocated.

In practice, the allocation of the evaluator-query budget across C C , L L , and K K depends on the task. Problems whose progress depends on discovering qualitatively different approaches, such as mathematical construction problems, benefit from larger C C , whereas problems dominated by incremental engineering refinement, such as GPU kernel optimization, benefit from larger L L . The local sample size K K should remain moderate: increasing it makes each committed step more reliable, but leaves fewer refinement steps under the same budget. Unless otherwise stated, we use C = 32 C=32 , L = 100 L=100 , and K = 16 K=16 as a default configuration.

While C C , L L , and K K govern how evaluated histories grow, the proposal constructor Φ \Phi governs which parts of that history are reused, since a trajectory’s history soon becomes too long and noisy to feed back in full. Because each proposal can build on several prior attempts rather than only the last, the history forms a directed graph rather than a chain. Φ \Phi selects which attempts to reuse through a graph-based variant of the PUCT rule [ 115 , 127 , 126 ] , favoring attempts that scored well or led to strong later attempts, while still giving promising but rarely used ones a chance to be revisited. It also folds in a running summary of recurring failures, such as compilation errors, verifier failures, or timeouts, to steer G G away from unproductive directions. In Methods, a Pólya-urn analysis together with ablations on C C , L L , and K K shows why these dimensions form a compact design space for scaling the loop, and Φ \Phi ’s construction and selection strategy are specified and ablated in full. We next turn to the discoveries SimpleTES makes across scientific domains, and then to how a language model can be trained for SimpleTES -based discovery.

### SimpleTES discovers effective algorithms to reduce quantum compilation overhead

Quantum computing is an increasingly important computing paradigm that stores and processes information via quantum-mechanical systems [ 99 ] . Whereas a classical bit has a definite value of either 0 or 1, a quantum bit, or qubit , can be in a coherent superposition of both states. By manipulating such states and creating correlations between qubits through entangling operations, quantum computers can obtain polynomial or exponential speedups for selected problem classes [ 125 , 50 , 35 , 52 , 13 , 37 , 84 , 7 ] . At the center of this paradigm is the quantum algorithm, which specifies how quantum operations transform input states into desired outputs. In the circuit model, a quantum algorithm is represented by a quantum circuit: an ordered sequence of elementary operations, called gates , applied to logical qubits [ 99 ] . One-qubit gates update the state of a single qubit, while two-qubit gates create interactions between a pair of qubits. A logical circuit may specify two-qubit gates between arbitrary pairs of logical qubits, but a real device can apply a direct two-qubit gate only when the corresponding physical qubits satisfy the hardware’s interaction constraints, thus creating a compilation problem of how to assign logical qubits to physical qubits and update that assignment over time so that every required two-qubit gate becomes executable. The additional operations introduced in this process are costly and error-prone, making overhead reduction the central optimization goal.

This optimization problem is computationally challenging and takes different forms on different hardware platforms [ 61 , 128 , 28 , 15 , 133 , 80 , 132 , 137 ] . We use SimpleTES to address two representative hardware settings, superconducting platforms and neutral-atom architectures. In superconducting devices, physical qubits occupy fixed chip locations and allowed two-qubit interactions form a sparse coupling graph; compilation must choose an initial layout and insert SWAP gates when interacting logical qubits are mapped to non-adjacent physical qubits [ 76 , 176 ] . In zoned neutral-atom devices, atoms are kept in a storage zone or moved to an entangling zone for Rydberg gates; compilation must choose placement snapshots whose transitions can be realized through AOD-mediated movement. Together, these settings contrast fixed-graph routing with geometry-limited atom placement and rearrangement, while retaining the same high-level goal of minimizing extra hardware work. We apply SimpleTES within fixed compilation scaffolds that isolate editable policy components, with necessary correctness checks applied. For superconducting circuits, SimpleTES edits the initial layout and online SWAP selection rule; candidate routers are compared against SABRE [ 76 ] and LightSABRE [ 176 ] , with added SWAP overhead as the cost. For zoned neutral-atom compilation, SimpleTES edits only the placer in a fixed scheduler–reuse-analyzer–placer–router pipeline, with the remaining modules held fixed; candidate placers are compared against a reuse-aware ZAC-style baseline [ 80 ] , with total execution time as the objective. Formal problem definitions, hardware constraints, and optimization objectives for both compilation tasks are given in Supplementary H.1 . Benchmark suites, baselines, and evaluation metrics are provided in Supplementary H.2 .

Across both settings, SimpleTES achieves substantial overhead reductions relative to established baselines, driven by a shared design principle: place important logical qubits near useful hardware locations before expensive routing or rearrangement becomes unavoidable. In superconducting routing, the best policy reduces topology-averaged added SWAP overhead by 21.7% relative to SABRE and 14.9% relative to LightSABRE , with the largest reductions on Q20 reaching 33.3% and 24.5%, respectively; it implements the shared principle by mapping high-traffic logical qubits toward high-degree physical qubits, refining the assignment by local search, and scoring online SWAPs using a broader candidate neighborhood, look-ahead information, and immediate gate executability ( Figure 2 b,c). In zoned neutral-atom compilation, the best placer discovered by SimpleTES reduces the geometric mean of total execution time by 33.2% relative to the ZAC-style baseline; it implements the same principle through the sequence of placement snapshots passed to the fixed AOD router, preserving relative qubit order, reducing travel distance without losing AOD parallelism, and refining placements through reverse-time passes ( Figure 2 d–f). Further details of the best discovered programs and the additional superconducting routing policy obtained using SimpleTES with Gemini are provided in Supplementary H.3 and H.4 .

### SimpleTES improves optimizers for fuel-efficient deep-space trajectories

Deep-space missions often reach distant targets by using planets as moving gravitational waypoints rather than flying directly. A gravity assist (GA) uses a planetary flyby to reshape the spacecraft’s heliocentric path with little propellant, whereas a deep-space maneuver (DSM) uses an engine burn to adjust the path [ 98 ] . Designing such missions is difficult because route choices and timing choices are tightly coupled: the optimizer must choose which bodies to encounter, which transfer branches to use, and where maneuvers may be inserted, while also setting launch and encounter epochs and maneuver variables [ 148 , 121 ] . We formulate this as an evaluation-driven discovery task in which each candidate produced by SimpleTES is an executable mission-design program. Given mission windows, a target body, allowed flyby bodies, and endpoint conditions, the program proposes complete multi-gravity-assist trajectories ( Figure 3 a); an independent patched-conic evaluator reconstructs the trajectory from ephemerides [ 38 ] , transfer arcs [ 62 ] , flybys [ 95 ] , DSMs, and endpoint costs, rejects constraint violations, and scores feasible designs by verified propulsive cost.

We benchmark SimpleTES on five historical mission-window benchmarks and a prospective Jupiter exploration window. For the historical benchmarks, the comparison is not to flown mission Δ ​ v \Delta v budgets, which incorporate operational choices, navigation margins, and mission-specific high-fidelity modeling. Instead, each reference trajectory is constructed from the historical encounter sequence and nominal timing, then evaluated under the same orbital-dynamics model, feasibility constraints, and cost definition used to score SimpleTES . Across the historical mission windows, the best verified trajectories found by the discovered optimizers reduce propulsive cost in every case, by 2.1–23.1% relative to these same-evaluator references ( Figure 3 b). In the prospective 2028–2030 Jupiter exploration window, where no encounter sequence is prescribed, the discovered optimizer synthesizes an evaluator-feasible no-DSM Earth–Venus–Earth–Earth–Jupiter trajectory reaching Jupiter in 2035 ( Figure 3 a).

Inspection of the evolved optimizers shows that the gains arise from reusable policies for coupled discrete–continuous mission design. The clearest sequence-level changes occur for Galileo and Rosetta ( Figure 3 c): the Galileo optimizer finds Earth–Venus–Venus–Venus–Earth–Jupiter rather than the reference Earth–Venus–Earth–Earth–Jupiter sequence, while the Rosetta optimizer finds Earth–Earth–Earth–67P rather than Earth–Earth–Mars–Earth–Earth–67P. For Mariner 10, Voyager 2, and Cassini, the programs independently recover the reference encounter order and lower cost by refining the continuous trajectory variables within that sequence, showing that improvements do not require a different encounter sequence. Across cases, the evolved programs generate and prioritize candidate encounter sequences, allocate finite computation to promising time-of-flight regions, explore multiple transfer branches, combine global search with local refinement of encounter epochs and maneuver variables, and retain DSMs only when they reduce verified cost. Together, these results show that the evolved optimizers allocate finite computation both across encounter sequences and within each sequence’s continuous design space.

### SimpleTES discovers better scientific algorithms

Scientific algorithms are the computational methods that solve scientific problems: optimization solvers, forecasters, and learned models whose quality is judged by task-specific scientific objectives. These algorithms are usually produced by hand, often after years of accumulated domain expertise and engineering. We apply SimpleTES to three such problems and find that the same evaluation-driven search procedure discovers algorithms that improve over strong baselines: a geometry-aware lasso path solver that preserves strict correctness guarantees while outperforming the expert-engineered glmnet on real datasets; a whole-brain neural-activity forecaster that outperforms a prior proprietary-model search result; and a single-cell RNA-seq denoising algorithm that generalizes across datasets. We discuss the first two in detail below. Full denoising results are reported in the Supplementary Information, where an algorithm evolved exclusively on the Pancreas dataset matches the previous state of the art on held-out PBMC ( 0.71 0.71 ) and establishes a new best on held-out Tabula Muris Senis Lung ( 0.74 0.74 versus 0.73 0.73 ) (Supplementary M ).

##### SimpleTES discovers a geometry-aware lasso solver that surpasses glmnet .

The lasso regularization path is a core computational primitive in high-dimensional statistics, used in cross-validation, model selection, and large-scale regression workflows from genomics to finance. The de facto solver, glmnet [ 39 ] , encodes more than a decade of expert coordinate-descent engineering: warm starts, sequential strong-rule screening [ 142 ] , active-set inner loops, KKT verification, and a precision-matched switch between the covariance ( p < 500 p<500 ) and naive residual ( p ≥ 500 p\geq 500 ) updates. We ask whether evaluation-driven search, starting from a faithful C++ port of glmnet itself, can produce a strictly faster solver while preserving the same correctness guarantees. The evaluator is exact: candidate programs are self-contained C++ binaries, and a candidate is valid only if its per- λ \lambda objective gap relative to sklearn ’s solution stays below 10 − 6 10^{-6} on a held-out problem, with any failure forcing the score to zero. Evolution operates exclusively on synthetic problems, scored by the geometric mean of solve times across 17 procedurally generated instances that span sample-to-feature ratio, design-matrix sparsity, active-set density, and feature correlation. Because no real dataset is ever exposed to the search, the gains reported on real data below reflect generalization rather than tuning to the test set.

Across the 11 real-world datasets in Supplementary Table 16 , none seen during the search, SimpleTES discovers a solver that is 2.17 × 2.17\times faster than glmnet on average and 14.08 × 14.08\times faster than sklearn , with every reported solution passing the held-out correctness check. What makes the discovered solver notable is not just raw speed but geometry awareness : it reads the shape of each problem and adapts its algorithm accordingly. Where glmnet applies coordinate descent uniformly, SimpleTES ’s solver detects the problem regime and switches solver family to match it. For moderate-dimensional, tall designs ( p ≤ 2000 p\leq 2000 and n ≥ p / 4 n\geq p/4 ) it abandons coordinate descent for an exact LARS homotopy solver that traces the regularization path analytically, updating the active set at each kink with rank-one inverse Gram updates [ 34 ] ; for wide or sparse designs ( p > 2000 p>2000 or n < p / 4 n<p/4 ) it retains coordinate descent with strong-rule screening. This geometry-dependent switching is exactly what the speedups track: the gains peak at 9.56 × 9.56\times over glmnet on the tall DNA design ( 1700 × 180 1700\times 180 , the only case with n ≫ p n\gg p ), where the LARS branch fires on a small active set, settle at 1.30 1.30 – 1.50 × 1.50\times on wide biological designs where p ≫ n p\gg n , and reach 1.36 1.36 – 1.76 × 1.76\times on large mixed-shape datasets such as RCV1 and Gisette. The vs- sklearn gains are largest on the biological datasets, up to 50.77 × 50.77\times on TCGA BRCA. In effect, the discovered program recombines two well-known but historically separate lasso solver families into a single geometry-aware policy that selects the right one for each input, arrived at from no prior beyond the starting code and the evaluator. That the speedups hold on unseen real data suggests it exploits genuine problem structure rather than fitting the synthetic surrogate distribution.

##### SimpleTES discovers new state-of-the-art predictor for Zebrafish whole-brain neural-activity.

Forecasting the neural activity of an entire vertebrate brain is a central challenge for understanding how brains turn sensory input and internal state into behavior [ 3 ] . Continuous whole-brain recording at single-cell resolution is limited by motion artifact [ 175 ] , photobleaching and phototoxicity [ 72 ] , and the trade-off between imaging speed and field-of-view [ 109 ] . Predictive models bridge this gap by learning from recorded activity and stimulus context to forecast future brain states. The Zebrafish Activity Prediction Benchmark (ZAPBench) [ 88 ] , built from a two-hour light-sheet recording of a larval zebrafish exposed to a sequence of visual stimuli, provides activity traces for 71,721 71{,}721 neurons over 7,879 7{,}879 time steps across nine stimulus conditions, with the task of predicting neural activities up to 32 32 steps ahead from only 4 4 steps of context. The strongest previously reported forecaster was discovered by Empirical Research Assistance (ERA) [ 9 ] , a tree-search method guided by a proprietary frontier model; ERA and SimpleTES differ in both the search algorithm and the underlying model, and we ask whether SimpleTES with an open-sourced model can discover competitive forecasters on the same task under similar computational limits.

SimpleTES achieves the strongest forecasting performance on ZAPBench, outperforming prior methods at every evaluated horizon ( Figure 4 d): its horizon-averaged test MAE of 0.0259 0.0259 at 32 steps improves over ERA ( 0.0283 0.0283 ; 8.5 % 8.5\% relative reduction) and the strongest ZAPBench baseline, the volumetric U-Net ( 0.0292 0.0292 ; 11.3 % 11.3\% relative reduction). Its whole-brain prediction closely matches both the spatial organization and magnitude of the recorded population response ( Figure 4 a); the neuron-by-time raster preserves the dominant temporal structure of the recorded activity ( Figure 4 b). The prediction error varies across stimulus conditions ( Figure 4 c), and grows over the forecast horizon ( Figure 4 e). The discovered program ( Figure 4 f) shows that SimpleTES improves performance by automatically identifying a compact, well-matched combination of forecasting modules and their parameterization. The decisive change is to reformulate the prediction target: rather than regressing absolute future activity, the network predicts a residual added back through an identity skip connection, echoing residual learning [ 53 ] and last-value normalization from NLinear-style models [ 168 ] . Around this residual predictor it adds an explicit linear-trend baseline, combines it with the nonlinear forecasts using per-horizon closed-form least-squares weights, and feeds the nonlinear predictor a rich per-neuron descriptor including first-order differences, window slopes, per-step population statistics, and data-driven identity features from each neuron’s low-rank SVD loadings and coupling to the brain-wide mean.

### SimpleTES improves AI foundations

Two important foundations of modern AI development are building efficient GPU kernels that make models run fast on real hardware ( Figure 5 a), and discovering scaling laws that help predict how model performance changes as training is scaled up ( Figure 5 d). Both have traditionally relied on expert manual design: GPU kernels are carefully optimized by systems engineers for specific hardware architectures and software stacks, while scaling laws are manually specified and fitted by researchers to predict how performance changes with model size, data size, compute, and other scaling variables. We apply SimpleTES to both classes of problem and find that the same evaluation-driven search procedure produces kernels that surpass expert Triton submissions and AI kernel-discovery baselines on real hardware, and scaling laws that extrapolate substantially better than the strongest agent baselines on the SLDBench benchmark.

##### GPU kernel optimization.

We apply SimpleTES to optimize the GPU kernels that modern AI systems rely on. SimpleTES starts from a simple PyTorch reference implementation and searches for a faster GPU kernel that preserves numerical correctness within a task-specific tolerance. We evaluate this ability on three representative operators. The first is TriMul, a triangular multiplicative update used in AlphaFold3-class protein-structure models [ 1 , 140 ] and the subject of an active GPUMode optimization competition [ 49 ] . The second is asymmetric matrix multiplication, an irregular-shape GEMM pattern in modern AI workloads, including small-batch inference, LoRA adapters, and query-similarity search. The third is batched cumulative sum, a primitive in LLM sampling and stream-compaction pipelines. Across all three tasks, SimpleTES discovers kernels on an NVIDIA H200, and the resulting implementations are evaluated without re-tuning on the target GPUs. We compare against both expert-engineered kernels and recent AI-driven kernel-discovery systems baselines that achieve previous SOTA efficiency [ 165 , 20 , 14 , 29 ] .

On TriMul, a kernel discovered by SimpleTES on H200 transfers directly to H100 without re-tuning, reaching a geometric-mean latency of 1.122 ms, surpassing both the AI baseline of 1.154 ms in Figure 5 c and the strongest expert-written Triton baseline of 1.140 ms on the GPUMode leaderboard (Supplementary Table 12 ). The same implementation achieves the fastest runtime on NVIDIA A100, NVIDIA H100, and AMD MI300, suggesting that the search found a portable implementation strategy rather than an H200-specific optimization (Supplementary Table 13 ). Inspection of the discovered implementation shows that the gain comes from jointly optimizing high-level structure and low-level kernel tuning. SimpleTES fuses layer normalization, gating, projection, and masking into a small number of Triton kernels, selects a mixed-precision path in which activations move in FP16 while reductions remain in FP32, and tunes tile sizes within the kernels to improve hardware utilization ( Figure 5 b). The asymmetric-matmul and batched-cumsum tasks further show that SimpleTES can specialize the implementation strategy to the input shape, rather than relying on a one-size-fits-all template for each operator. On asymmetric matrix multiplication, the largest gains occur on highly uneven matrix shapes, where SimpleTES achieves up to 4.01 × 4.01\times speedup over the PyTorch FP32 reference and reduces runtime by 65.6% relative to CUDA Agent on the case with the largest improvement ( Figure 5 c). Batched cumulative sum provides a clearer example of shape-dependent implementation: the discovered kernel selects between single-tile and multi-tile execution paths according to workload size. This implementation outperforms NVIDIA’s hand-tuned CUB DeviceSegmentedScan on four of the six evaluated shapes and reaches 6.06 × 6.06\times speedup over the PyTorch reference. CUB remains only marginally faster on the two smallest shapes, where its fixed segmented-scan strategy is already well matched to the input. These case studies show that SimpleTES can automatically discover optimization strategies that are typically hand-engineered in optimized GPU libraries. Detailed shape definitions are provided in the Supplementary Tables 9 , 10 and 11 .

##### Scaling-law discovery.

We evaluate SimpleTES on four tasks from the SLDBench benchmark [ 79 , 78 ] , covering parallel scaling [ 26 ] , domain-mixture scaling [ 163 ] , learning-rate/batch-size co-scaling [ 77 ] , and U-shape compute scaling [ 157 ] . The task is to discover a symbolic law f θ f_{\theta} together with a per-setting fitting procedure that extrapolates accurately to held-out larger-scale configurations, where each configuration specifies the scale variables and task-specific control variables used for fitting and evaluation. Success is measured by the extrapolation coefficient of determination ( R 2 R^{2} ) on each task’s extrapolation split. To isolate the effect of the search strategy, we use the same initialization, task instructions, and evaluator as the SLDAgent baseline [ 79 ] : the initial program is a naive power-law function paired with a BFGS optimizer. Across the four tasks ( Figure 5 d, Supplementary Table 14 ), SimpleTES achieves the best average R 2 R^{2} of 0.674, exceeding the strongest baseline (SLDAgent with GPT-5, 0.572) and every other configuration tested. The advantage is sharpest on the hardest extrapolative settings: SimpleTES achieves the highest R 2 R^{2} on the U-shape compute-scaling task ( u_shape ; − 0.008 -0.008 , versus − 0.232 -0.232 for the next-best baseline) and on the learning-rate/batch-size co-scaling task ( lr&bsz ; 0.712 versus 0.611 for SLDAgent+o4-mini), where most agents obtain negative test-set R 2 R^{2} and fail to extrapolate. The discovered law on lr&bsz produces a hyperparameter recommendation only 0.058% above the true optimum at a 1B-parameter, 100B-token target setting, corresponding to a 10 × \times extrapolation in model size and a 100 × \times extrapolation in data size (Supplementary K ).

These two task families share an interface—an initial solution and a queryable evaluator—and differ only in what the evaluator measures (wall-clock runtime under a correctness tolerance for kernels; held-out extrapolation R 2 R^{2} for scaling laws). What unites them substantively is that both produce artifacts that AI development itself depends on: faster kernels reduce the cost of training and serving every downstream model, and more accurate scaling laws improve the decisions made before training begins. The same evaluation-driven scaling principle that surpasses expert Triton kernels on a competition leaderboard also discovers symbolic loss laws that extrapolate where strong agent baselines do not.

### Post-training the LLM for SimpleTES leads to new mathematical discoveries

### Discussion

SimpleTES reframes open-ended scientific discovery as a problem of structured scaling: how evaluator queries should be distributed across competing lines of inquiry, and how the resulting experience should guide subsequent attempts. Its design tuple ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) organizes this process across independent exploration, iterative refinement, local selection, and the selective reuse of evaluated history. Applying this same procedure and a single open model to 28 open-ended problems across five domains, while changing only the evaluator, SimpleTES reaches new state-of-the-art solutions across objectives ranging from exact mathematical verification and physical simulation to held-out prediction error and empirical hardware timing. More important than any individual record, these results show that evaluator queries become substantially more powerful when they are structured so that exploration and refinement can compound. Together, they establish structured evaluation-driven scaling as a general mechanism for discovery.

An important feature of these results is what SimpleTES deliberately holds fixed. For the cross-domain results above, each proposal is produced by a single call to the same LLM, without multi-agent coordination, multi-step agentic planning, model ensembles, or online parameter updates. In contrast, some existing discovery systems tend to combine evaluator-guided search with one or more of these components [ 100 , 6 , 73 , 155 , 165 ] , making it difficult to isolate the contribution of evaluation-driven scaling itself. By keeping the proposer minimal and making the allocation of evaluator queries explicit, SimpleTES shows that structured scaling is independently powerful: a single open model can match or surpass systems built around substantially more complex machinery. This does not imply that stronger models or more capable agents would provide no additional benefit; either could be incorporated into the same framework. Rather, the results establish that such complexity is not a prerequisite for discovery at this level and that the organization of the evaluation loop is itself an important source of capability.

The same structure also creates practical routes for scaling the discovery loop more efficiently. The C C trajectories can proceed independently, while the K K candidates conditioned on the same proposal can be produced in a single batched LLM request, exposing substantial parallelism and avoiding repeated processing of shared context. For instance, under matched evaluator-query budgets and runtime concurrency, SimpleTES reaches OpenEvolve’s whole-run best score with an 11.8 × 11.8\times time-to-solution speedup on the third autocorrelation inequality problem, and an 18.0 × 18.0\times speedup on the sum-difference problem (Supplementary Figure 8 ). The independence of trajectories also permits early pruning where trajectories can be stopped early on if they are not promising. For instance, under the most aggressive setting that retains only one trajectory after 25% refinement steps, the trajectory that ultimately produced the best solution survives in 55% problems, while relative score degradation remains below 0.01 % 0.01\% in most configurations and below 0.03 % 0.03\% in all configurations (Supplementary Figure 7 ). This behavior is task-dependent, with pruning nearly lossless on circle-packing tasks but less reliable on the autocorrelation and Erdős problems, where early scores are weaker indicators of eventual trajectory quality (Supplementary Table 4 ). These results motivate adaptive allocation policies that redirect evaluator queries using not only intermediate scores but also trajectory growth and semantic signals.

The power of evaluation-driven scaling also makes evaluator fidelity a central concern. Because SimpleTES optimizes the evaluator’s score directly, it cannot inherently distinguish a genuine improvement in the intended objective from an implementation loophole that produces a higher score. Such exploits emerge even without access to the evaluator’s source code. In mathematical construction tasks, the model exploits numerical tolerances, for example by producing circle packings with overlaps that fall just below the rejection threshold; in GPU-kernel optimization, it discovers more direct exploits, including caching outputs across timed runs, manipulating timing measurements, and corrupting the reference baseline (Supplementary D.3 ). We address these failures by independently re-verifying reported results and manually hardening evaluators as new vulnerabilities appear. This preserves the validity of our reported discoveries, but the process is reactive: each patch invites the next exploit, and this approach will not scale to objectives that cannot be checked cheaply. A durable solution is to make the evaluator itself part of what is optimized, learning or co-evolving it alongside the search so that the surrogate remains aligned with the true objective.

Even with robust evaluators, the present framework remains limited to problems for which feedback can be obtained automatically and inexpensively enough to support repeated queries. Many important scientific objectives fall outside this setting because their evaluation is subjective, experimentally costly, or available only through slow physical or wet-lab measurements. In addition, the evaluator-query budget is divided across C C , L L , and K K using fixed values, although our analyses show that the most effective allocation depends on both the task and the evolving state of the search. SimpleTES also relies primarily on continuous, fine-grained scores to guide refinement, making it less naturally suited to problems with sparse or binary feedback, such as formal theorem proving, where near-misses and unproductive attempts may receive indistinguishable scores. Extending evaluation-driven scaling to these settings will require adaptive allocation policies, richer semantic feedback extracted from failed attempts, partial proofs, and textual critiques, and interfaces to higher-fidelity simulators, laboratory robotics, or real-world measurements. Together, these advances could enable discovery loops that allocate effort dynamically and learn from heterogeneous forms of scientific feedback while preserving reliable verification.

### Methods

#### The evaluation-driven discovery loop

Recent studies on test-time scaling (TTS) improve model performance by increasing test-time computation, such as using more reasoning tokens [ 96 ] , multi-turn sampling [ 60 ] , or agentic workflows with tool use and search [ 24 , 27 ] . For scientific discovery, cycles of trial-and-error guided by external feedback are the foundation of breakthroughs. This motivates a central question: whether and how the evaluation-driven loop can be effectively scaled up. This form of scaling focuses on problems where an evaluation surrogate is both necessary and available , ruling out problems whose evaluation is purely subjective or currently intractable.

The evaluation-driven loop can be formalized as follows. Given a problem instruction x 0 ∈ 𝒳 x_{0}\in\mathcal{X} in the text space, we aim to discover a solution y ∈ 𝒴 y\in\mathcal{Y} that maximizes a true underlying objective. In real-world scientific discovery, this true objective is rarely directly accessible, so the discovery process relies on an explicit, queryable surrogate evaluator V : 𝒴 → ℝ × ℳ V\colon\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M} . For a candidate solution y y , the evaluator returns V ⁡ ( y ) = ( r , m ) V(y)=(r,m) , where r ∈ ℝ r\in\mathbb{R} is a scalar score, and m ∈ ℳ m\in\mathcal{M} contains auxiliary feedback or metadata, such as verifier messages, error traces, or other task-specific information. With access to this evaluator, the question can be stated precisely as: whether and how scaling the number of evaluation queries N N enables the discovery of a solution with a high score r r , which is expected to also perform well under the true objective.

The discovery process is controlled by a policy π \pi equipped with a language model G : 𝒳 → 𝒴 G\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y} 1 1 1 We fix G G to a single LLM, as we aim to study the scaling effect of evaluation queries. Nevertheless, it can be replaced by more complex designs, including model ensembles [ 100 , 73 ] , tool-using agentic workflows [ 162 , 122 , 10 , 118 , 24 , 27 ] , and trainable test-time-adapted models [ 165 , 155 , 178 ] . . Specifically, π \pi is initialized from a solution y 0 y_{0} , which may be a naive baseline or an existing strong solution, with ( r 0 , m 0 ) = V ⁡ ( y 0 ) (r_{0},m_{0})=V(y_{0}) being the initial score and feedback. Whenever the policy needs to, it can create a new proposal x n x_{n} , which may include instructions, historical solutions and feedback, statistics from past attempts, and any other information needed by G G to generate the next solution y n y_{n} . Once generated, y n y_{n} is evaluated by V V , and the resulting node ( y n , r n , m n ) ∈ 𝒴 × ℝ × ℳ (y_{n},r_{n},m_{n})\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M} is returned to the policy for constructing future proposals. This process continues until the evaluator-query budget N N is exhausted, and the highest-scoring solution is returned.

Notice that practical policies can be, and often are, asynchronous: multiple generation or evaluation jobs may be launched simultaneously, results may arrive out of order, and the policy may act before all earlier jobs have completed. This makes the space of possible policies extremely broad, ranging from simple refinement loops to complex evolutionary or agentic systems. A comprehensive review of existing designs of π \pi can be found in Supplementary B.1 . While these methods leverage evaluators in different ways, they often treat feedback as one component of a broader search procedure, rather than explicitly studying the scaling effect of the evaluation-driven loop itself. Evaluation-driven scaling instead asks what happens when the feedback-driven discovery loop is scaled up through more evaluator queries, rather than only scaling generation-side computation such as reasoning length, sampling count, or agent turns.

##### Remark on V V .

The assumption of an evaluator surrogate does not guarantee its perfect alignment with the true objective. Depending on the nature of the problem, the fidelity of the evaluator V V exists on a spectrum. It can be the exact metric we care about, such as a mathematical verifier ensuring the strict correctness of a construction, or a program counting the exact number of circles packed in a given square; It can be an empirical estimation of the metric, such as timing a GPU kernel on a subset of test cases to approximate its overall execution speed. It can also be a heuristic proxy , such as a regression loss evaluated on a limited training dataset, which potentially correlates with the true generalization capability. This inherent discrepancy between the surrogate evaluator and the true objective can lead to various forms of reward hacking [ 5 , 41 , 104 ] or overfitting [ 22 , 111 ] , challenges that we systematically analyze below.

#### Scaling the loop with ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi)

The key question is how evaluator queries should be used effectively. A naive best-of- N N policy spends the entire budget on independently sampled candidates, failing to use evaluator feedback to guide later attempts. A sequential refinement policy, on the other hand, uses feedback to improve later candidates, but commits the search to a single trajectory and can become trapped by early choices. Recent approaches demonstrate the power of iterative discovery systems that combine generation, evaluation, and refinement. Building on this evidence, this section asks a fundamental design question: how should evaluator queries be organized to use feedback most effectively?

We introduce SimpleTES , a simple algorithmic framework for scaling the evaluation-driven discovery loop at test time. The key idea is to organize evaluator queries through a compact design space: C C independent trajectories provide global exploration, L L committed refinement steps accumulate feedback within each trajectory, K K local candidates are evaluated before each commitment, and Φ \Phi maps the committed history into the next proposal. We present the pseudo-code of SimpleTES in Algorithm 1 , with its design space and analysis of each parameter specified below. We also discuss the theoretical insights for these parameter designs in Supplementary C .

##### Terminology.

A trajectory is a sequence of L L refinement steps that starts from the initial node ( y 0 , r 0 , m 0 ) (y_{0},r_{0},m_{0}) and accumulates evaluated nodes into a set S S . Each evaluated tuple ( y , r , m ) ∈ S (y,r,m)\in S is called a node , representing a solution along with its score and metadata. The context construction mapping Φ \Phi selects which historical nodes from S S to include in the next generation proposal x = Φ ⁡ ( S ) x=\Phi(S) . By default, SimpleTES allows each generation to condition on multiple historical nodes rather than only the immediate predecessor, enabling flexible recombination of successful patterns across the trajectory.

##### From sequential refinement to independent search.

To understand the design choice of SimpleTES , we start with a simple, straightforward evaluation-driven scaling policy: sequential refinement . Specifically, it generates a candidate, evaluates it, uses all historical feedback to generate a better one, and repeats. π seq \pi_{\text{seq}} corresponds to a special case in Definition 0.1 parameterized by ( 1 , L , 1 , Φ ) (1,L,1,\Phi) .

Yet sequential refinement has a fundamental limitation. Open-ended problems require multidimensional coverage: a high-quality solution must simultaneously satisfy multiple criteria (correctness, efficiency, generality, etc.). But refinements are path-dependent: the direction of early attempts largely determines the space of subsequent improvements. This mismatch creates a “Matthew Effect” where early progress in one dimension attracts further refinement to that same dimension, starving alternatives and trapping the search around local optima.

To overcome this limitation, SimpleTES introduces a simple additional axis: global width C C . Specifically, we run C C independent trajectories in parallel, each maintaining its own history and exploring possible directions for improvement separately. By enabling independent exploration, global width increases the chance that at least one trajectory starts from sufficiently diverse early attempts, allowing later refinements to compound into qualitatively better solutions.

For readers interested in a formal justification of this effect, we provide a mathematical model and theorem in Supplementary C . Here, we empirically demonstrate the phenomenon by applying SimpleTES to two mathematical problems under different combinations of C C and L L . As shown in Figure 7 (left), increasing L L initially improves performance but quickly saturates, consistent with the lock-in effect of a single refinement trajectory. In contrast, increasing C C provides a clear benefit by diversifying the committed histories explored under the same feedback-driven loop. This observation motivates global width as a key dimension of SimpleTES : before simply refining deeper, the policy should allocate evaluator queries to multiple independent trajectories.

##### From single samples to local batches.

The introduction of C C is due to the nature of the problems, whereas the introduction of K K , the local sample size, is due to the nature of the LLM. Even when a trajectory has identified a promising direction, a single call to the LLM can still produce a weak, noisy, or failed candidate. If such a candidate is immediately committed to the trajectory history, its errors may affect all subsequent refinements. This creates a local commitment risk at every refinement step.

Within each search trajectory, SimpleTES evaluates a batch of K K candidates for each proposal x x and adds only the highest-scoring one to the history. Under a fixed evaluator-query budget, introducing local sample size changes the allocation from ( C , L , 1 , Φ ) (C,L,1,\Phi) to approximately ( C , L / K , K , Φ ) (C,L/K,K,\Phi) . The allocation between L L and K K creates a trade-off: a larger K K improves the quality of each committed step, but it reduces the number of refinement steps. A smaller local sample size K K , on the other hand, makes the trajectory vulnerable to local generation noise that hurts future refinement.

As shown in Figure 7 , increasing K K from 1 1 to a moderate value consistently improves performance, indicating that local greedy selection helps prevent weak samples from entering the trajectory history. However, when K K becomes too large and leaves only a small refinement depth L L , the gains may saturate or even reverse, as the trajectory no longer has sufficient room to accumulate feedback over time. This motivates local sample size K K as another key dimension of SimpleTES : evaluator queries within a trajectory should balance the number of refinement steps with the reliability of each committed step.

##### From full history to compressed context.

The discussion above focuses on how evaluator queries are allocated, which gives the three scaling dimensions ( C , L , K ) (C,L,K) . This allocation view abstracts away another practical question: how the feedback accumulated along a trajectory is presented to the LLM. With an idealized LLM that has unlimited context, perfect attention, and the ability to extract all useful patterns from prior attempts, the policy could simply expose the entire committed history to it. Thus, Φ \Phi would not affect the allocation analysis above.

Real language models, however, are not ideal in this sense. The committed history of a trajectory can quickly become long, redundant, and noisy, containing solutions, scores, error messages, implementation details, and partial insights. Query construction is therefore a compression problem: the policy must convert the trajectory-local history into a generation proposal that fits the model’s capacity while preserving the feedback most useful for the next refinement step. We formalize this as a mapping Φ : FinSet ⁡ ( 𝒴 × ℝ × ℳ ) → 𝒳 , \displaystyle\Phi:\mathrm{FinSet}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M})\to\mathcal{X}, (4) where each node ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) contains a committed solution, its scalar score, and auxiliary metadata. The design of Φ \Phi involves two sub-problems: history selection , which decides which previous nodes should be exposed to the LLM, and prompt formatting , which decides how these nodes should be presented. We discuss concrete instantiations below.

Together, SimpleTES provides a simple but effective abstraction for scaling evaluation-driven discovery loops. The design space ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) isolates the core dimensions of evaluation-driven scaling, including diverse exploration, feedback-driven refinement, and local greedy selection. This compact formulation gives a clean interface for studying scaling behavior while remaining practical across diverse scientific discovery problems.

#### Implementation details

This subsection specifies the implementation details of SimpleTES . Due to the independence of trajectories, we omit the trajectory index c c below. Each trajectory is exposed to Φ \Phi only a finite set S ⊂ FinSet ⁡ ( 𝒴 × ℝ × ℳ ) S\subset\mathrm{FinSet}(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{M}) of historical nodes ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) . For clarity, we append necessary runtime metadata in m m , including the proposal index for each node, the return index, and other lightweight bookkeeping information, such as which historical nodes were selected for context, how often each node has been selected, summarized failure patterns, and optional reflections. This information is used in the definition of Φ \Phi and does not affect the algorithm design space.

##### Proposal created with historical nodes.

The main role of Φ \Phi is to decide which previously explored experiences should be presented in a new proposal that can maximize the potential result of this new solution. This is achieved by deciding which nodes in S S should be included in x = Φ ⁡ ( S ) x=\Phi(S) . Our default approach is RPUCG , a graph-based extension of PUCT [ 126 , 115 , 127 ] . Whenever a new solution is generated from a prompt that conditions on certain historical nodes, the implementation stores parent–child links among the corresponding nodes in S S . For a node indexed by i i , let Ch ⁡ ( i ) \mathrm{Ch}(i) denote all nodes that have included node i i in their proposal (all nodes that are inspired by node i i up to now). For each node, we maintain a propagated value U i = max ⁡ ( r i , γ ​ max j ∈ Ch ⁡ ( i ) ​ U j ) , \displaystyle U_{i}=\max\left(r_{i},\;\gamma\max_{j\in\mathrm{Ch}(i)}U_{j}\right), (5) where γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in(0,1] is a discount factor. The second term is ignored if Ch ⁡ ( i ) \mathrm{Ch}(i) is empty.

Intuitively, an experience is valuable either because (1) it already has a high evaluator score (so it should be able to inspire a similarly good solution), or (2) because its solution is inspiring and has led to strong descendants before. RPUCG balances the two reasons by the following formula: RPUCG ⁡ ( i ) = U i + λ ​ ρ i ​ 1 + | S | 1 + n i , \displaystyle\mathrm{RPUCG}(i)=U_{i}+\lambda\rho_{i}\frac{\sqrt{1+|S|}}{1+n_{i}}, (6) where λ \lambda controls exploration, ρ i \rho_{i} is the relative prior of node i i based on its score percentile within the current S S , and n i n_{i} is the number of times i i has been previously included in the context of a proposal.

The first term favors nodes with strong propagated value, while the second is an exploration term that allows unexplored nodes (whose n i n_{i} will be small) to be included.

To reduce redundancy, Φ \Phi selects historical nodes greedily according to RPUCG ⁡ ( i ) \mathrm{RPUCG}(i) , while excluding the one-hop neighbors. We use multiple historical nodes for context construction, in contrast to sequential refinement and prior work [ 155 , 165 ] , which condition on only a single node. We use RPUCG as the default selector in the main paper; alternative selectors are described in Methods.

##### Prompt construction.

After selecting historical nodes, Φ \Phi formats the next proposal x x for G G . Prompt design can substantially affect performance. Prior systems such as [ 6 , 155 , 165 ] often use complex instructions with task-specific hints and expert-designed heuristics. Such hints may improve performance, but they are difficult to curate and may not transfer across tasks. In pilot experiments, removing these hints from [ 155 ] and using only a plain instruction caused a substantial performance drop, suggesting that prompt construction is itself an important design problem.

SimpleTES adopts a minimalist prompt strategy and moves most adaptation into the search procedure. We avoid expert-crafted hints and manually specified error patterns. Each prompt contains four types of information: 1. the plain task instruction x 0 x_{0} ;

2. evaluation configurations, such as timeout and resource limits;

3. selected historical nodes from S S , including their scores, evaluator feedback m m , and concise summaries of useful observations;

4. optional automatically accumulated signals, such as repeated exceptions, timeout patterns, missing imports such as numpy , package information from requirements.txt , LLM-based reflections over committed winners, or validated artifacts that can serve as warm starts for constructive tasks. We ablate those design choices in Supplementary F .

These components add little direct human prior knowledge. Instead, they are accumulated from experience during search and exposed through the same interface x = Φ ⁡ ( S ) x=\Phi(S) . Figure 6 compares the task-instruction prompts used by [ 155 ] and SimpleTES .

##### Best-solution restart.

One of the advantages of the SimpleTES is its design choice of initial solution y 0 y_{0} , which can be a naive solution or an existing strong result. Therefore, a natural way to further scale up evaluation-driven loops is to start from solution obtained from previous SimpleTES runs. We refer to this process as best-solution restart strategy, where after a complete run finishes, we use the best discovered solution to initialize another identical run: y 0 ← arg ⁡ max ( y , r , m ) ∈ S ⁡ r . y_{0}\leftarrow\arg\max_{(y,r,m)\in S}r. This will reset the trajectory histories and all implementation bookkeeping. Notice that, empirically, we observe that most continual best-solution restarts will saturate; e.g., the second or third restart run could never have improved on the initial solution. The saturation indicates that the underlying mechanism of best-solution restart remains underexplored, and therefore, we do not include this design in our main SimpleTES design space. Throughout our experiments, we only run one additional best-solution restart unless otherwise specified.

##### Asynchronous execution.

Algorithm 1 describes the logical search process, while the runtime executes generation and evaluation asynchronously. Each call to Φ ⁡ ( S ) \Phi(S) creates one logical local batch for one trajectory. All K K candidates in the batch share the same proposal x x , and the trajectory updates its S S only after the batch has finished. Thus, the trajectory-level search semantics remain synchronized, even though generation and evaluation jobs may run concurrently and return out of order.

The implementation uses two worker pools, one for generation and one for evaluation, connected by bounded queues. A local batch can be dispatched either as one K K -sample generation request or as K K separate one-sample requests. The two modes are semantically identical because they share the same proposal x x and are reduced by the same local best-of- K K commit rule. The streamed mode improves overlap between generation and evaluation, while the batched mode reduces request overhead.

To prevent the runtime from drifting too far ahead of the policy state, we apply trajectory-level backpressure. Each active trajectory may have only a bounded number of unresolved local batches. With the default setting, a trajectory cannot launch a second local batch until the previous one has resolved, recovering strict trajectory synchronization. Increasing this bound allows deeper pipelining. The generation and evaluation queues are also physically bounded; when they are saturated, submission blocks rather than discarding work. If no new proposal can be scheduled, the scheduler waits for either generation or evaluation progress. The runtime does not evict already admitted generation or evaluation jobs; control is exerted at admission time.

##### Evaluation engineering.

Executing thousands of untrusted LLM-generated programs requires strict isolation. To reduce cost, we do not rely on cloud sandboxing services such as E2B [ 33 ] or Daytona [ 30 ] ; instead, each SimpleTES run is evaluated on a single compute node. Each evaluation is executed in a fresh subprocess with process-group timeouts and memory limits, preventing infinite loops and resource leaks from affecting the broader system. For more complex tasks, we additionally enforce container-level isolation through Docker, with networking disabled and temporary directories partitioned to prevent cross-run contamination.

To mitigate reward hacking, we implement independent score verification. The system does not trust metrics reported by generated code. Instead, an outer secure process independently recomputes final scores using isolated test data. This separation between generated programs and evaluation data prevents models from exploiting hardcoded outputs or overfitting to exposed test cases, improving the reliability of the reported results.

#### Learning to scale the loop

The SimpleTES framework facilitates the generation of a massive amount of structured, high-quality search trajectories. All these histories are a natural form of supervision on how to learn from prior experience for future improvements, analogous to how a human researcher’s experience accumulates into deeper scientific expertise. In this section, we propose a training paradigm that enables the model to systematically learn from trial-and-error experiences, thereby catalyzing subsequent scientific discoveries.

From the perspective of reinforcement learning [ 51 ] , evaluation-driven scaling appears deceptively simple: every proposed solution candidate receives evaluator feedback, and one might therefore train the model to optimize each step according to its immediate reward. This training paradigm has been adopted by a few existing attempts [ 155 , 165 ] . However, this is not a short-horizon task in which each candidate should consistently achieve optimal scores; it is a long-horizon process in which early attempts may be valuable precisely because they expose failure modes, diversify the search, or create useful starting points for later refinement. A model trained only to prefer candidates with high instantaneous scores may therefore become overly conservative, missing the exploratory behaviors that attempt to jump out from the local optimum and enable later breakthroughs. Consequently, the goal of the post-training process should shift from maximizing the quality of every intermediate solution to developing an awareness of the topology of evaluation-driven scaling: to learn how evaluator feedback should shape the trajectory of future attempts.

##### Trajectory-Level post-training.

In this paper, we post-train models at the trajectory level, rather than the instance level, to align the optimization objective with the long-horizon nature of evaluation scaling. Specifically, we conceptualize the entire trajectory as a “rollout” step, with each node–comprising a reasoning process and the proposed solution–representing an action. We deliberately bypass ALL intermediate rewards, and instead backpropagate the ultimate trajectory-level performance (i.e., the maximum score achieved across the entire history) to all constituent actions. By intentionally discarding myopic signals, this training objective becomes unbiased, ensuring that the model optimizes for the global breakthrough that aligns with the intention of scientific discovery, rather than local, potentially non-optimal, metrics.

##### Credit assignment.

The fundamental challenge in trajectory-level post-training lies in the design of the credit assignment mechanism. In terms of the concrete design choices, one may directly use the absolute terminate score similar to REINFORCE [ 156 ] , use a relative advantage similar to GRPO [ 120 ] , or even simply a binary reward similar to iterative rejection fine-tuning (IRFT) [ 144 ] . For simplicity, in this paper, we instantiate our training with the IRFT approach by assigning w = 1 w=1 to trajectories with the top R % R\% scores for each task, and w = 0 w=0 to the remaining, which focuses the model’s learning capacity on high-quality trajectories. To improve data efficiency, we truncate and discard all nodes in a trajectory subsequent to the point where the maximal score first appears. This simple post-train approach turns out to be effective, as we will present in Supplementary D.2 . We leave studies on more data-efficient approaches to future work.

##### Engineering details.

In practice, we adopt additional engineering designs to ensure robust convergence and computational efficiency. First, due to the high cost of search sampling, we maintain a persistent replay buffer, i.e., instead of relying exclusively on on-policy rollouts that are discarded after a single training iteration, the historical nodes 𝒟 \mathcal{D} are progressively accumulated and resampled, providing richer data for training. Besides, to avoid the cold-start problem, we set a larger rollout size C ^ \hat{C} in the first iteration to populate the replay buffer with a diverse set of trajectories. Second, rather than maintaining a static threshold, we dynamically decrease the top-tier ratio R R as training continues. This adaptive mechanism prevents the volume of training data from scaling uncontrollably and ensures that the model is progressively trained on only the most elite trajectories as its performance improves.

### B Related Work

#### B.1 Existing Evaluation-Driven Discovery Methods

Existing evaluation-driven discovery systems can be viewed as different instantiations of the TES policy π \pi introduced in Methods. Given an evaluated history of records ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) , where V ⁡ ( y ) = ( r , m ) V(y)=(r,m) , the policy decides when to spend the next evaluator query, how to construct the next proposal x n x_{n} for the generator G G , and how the resulting feedback is stored for future proposals. This perspective separates three design choices that are often entangled in prior work: the allocation of the evaluation budget N N , the construction of proposals from historical feedback, and the location of adaptation, which may reside in an external archive, a prompt-level controller, the optimized artifact itself, or the generator parameters. It also clarifies how existing methods relate to the compact design space ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) studied in SimpleTES : most prior systems introduce sophisticated controllers or training mechanisms, whereas our focus is to isolate how scaling the evaluation-driven loop itself affects discovery.

##### AlphaEvolve [ 100 ] .

AlphaEvolve is an asynchronous evolutionary method with a frozen generator. Rather than following a single refinement chain, it spends the evaluation budget by repeatedly launching mutation jobs whenever sampling and evaluation capacity is available. Each proposal x n x_{n} is constructed from fixed problem context, one or more parent programs retrieved from an evolutionary database, previously evaluated solutions, rendered scores and execution feedback, and instructions asking the LLM to produce Search / Replace edits. Thus, the historical records ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) are exposed to G G through an archive-driven prompting rule. After evaluation, the new record is inserted into the database, and future proposals resurface diverse high-performing programs using an island- or MAP-Elites-style archive policy. In the TES view, AlphaEvolve mainly adapts through the external archive and its resurfacing controller, while G G itself remains fixed. Its strong empirical performance comes with a complex policy π \pi whose precise archive-sampling heuristic is only partially specified in the public description.

##### OpenEvolve [ 6 ] .

OpenEvolve is an open-source implementation of AlphaEvolve-style evolutionary code discovery. It also treats TES as an asynchronous pipeline: generation, evaluation, and database updates proceed continuously rather than through synchronized refinement rounds. The proposal x n x_{n} is constructed from the problem description, selected evolved programs, evaluator scores, execution artifacts, and error feedback. OpenEvolve further supports LLM ensembles, multi-objective evaluation, MAP-Elites archives, island migration, checkpointing, and visualization. In our notation, its policy state is dominated by an external program database and the controller metadata used to sample parents, elites, diverse inspirations, and exploratory candidates. Compared with SimpleTES , OpenEvolve explores a much richer engineering space, but this also makes it harder to isolate which gains come from scaling evaluator queries N N and which come from archive heuristics, prompt engineering, or system-level design.

##### ShinkaEvolve [ 73 ] .

ShinkaEvolve makes the policy π \pi more explicitly adaptive. At each generation event, the controller first selects an island and then constructs a mutation proposal from that island’s archive. The proposal x n x_{n} may include a primary parent, inspiration programs sampled from top-performing and random archive entries, public performance metrics, textual evaluator feedback, and meta-scratchpad recommendations. The generator is also chosen from an LLM ensemble with sampled decoding settings, and the candidate may be requested as a diff edit, full rewrite, or crossover mutation. Before spending expensive evaluator queries, ShinkaEvolve can reject proposals using embedding-based novelty checks and an optional LLM novelty judge. After V V returns ( r , m ) (r,m) , the method updates per-island archives, offspring counts, model-selection statistics, and a meta-scratchpad summarizing recently successful strategies. Thus, adaptation occurs both through archive memory and through a controller that changes which model, parent, and mutation operator are used. In TES terms, ShinkaEvolve uses evaluation feedback not only to select better solutions, but also to continually reshape the proposal distribution induced by π \pi .

##### ThetaEvolve [ 155 ] .

ThetaEvolve uses batched decision events rather than purely asynchronous evolutionary updates. At each step, it samples many parents from a large program database and issues a batch of generator calls to a single LLM. Its proposal construction is comparatively lean: x n x_{n} typically contains task meta-information, code-replacement rules, and a sampled parent program, making the method close to iterative refinement over a large external database. Before expensive verification, ThetaEvolve performs early checks for malformed outputs, compile or runtime failures, invalid solutions, and duplicate programs. Valid children are evaluated by V V , inserted into the database, and used to reorganize future sampling. In its RL variant, the same batch also updates the generator parameters θ \theta using GRPO-style optimization and reward shaping. Therefore, unlike archive-only methods, ThetaEvolve can move part of the policy state into G G itself: successful mutation patterns are internalized by the model parameters rather than remaining only in the external history. This differs from our main setting, where G G is fixed in order to study the scaling behavior of evaluation-driven search more directly.

##### TTT-Discover [ 165 ] .

TTT-Discover explicitly combines evaluator-guided search with online model adaptation. Although the original paper describes the environment state as the current candidate solution, under the TES decomposition the reuse buffer, reuse statistics, and model parameters are all part of the effective policy state. At each rollout, the policy constructs a proposal by warm-starting from a previously discovered solution selected from a reuse buffer. The selection rule is PUCT-style: it uses rank-based priors, expansion counts, and the best reward achieved by descendants of a reused state. Prior actions can also be converted into natural-language context and inserted into the next proposal x n x_{n} . After evaluation, the new attempt is added to the buffer, reuse statistics are updated, and the model is trained online with an entropic RL objective that emphasizes high-reward discoveries. Thus, TTT-Discover is a clear example where TES feedback changes both the external memory used by π \pi and the generator parameters used by G G . Relative to SimpleTES , it places more emphasis on test-time training, whereas our main algorithm asks how far one can go by organizing evaluator queries with a fixed generator.

##### AdaEvolve [ 23 ] .

AdaEvolve introduces a hierarchical controller for deciding how to spend evaluator queries. Each iteration first chooses an island using a bandit rule, then decides whether that island should explore or exploit, and finally constructs a mutation proposal. In exploration mode, the policy samples parents more uniformly and pairs them with diverse inspirations, asking for more orthogonal changes. In exploitation mode, it samples stronger parents and asks for targeted refinements. When global stagnation is detected, a separate meta-guidance model analyzes the problem specification, evaluator, and recent failed attempts, then produces a high-level tactic that is injected into future proposals. After evaluation, AdaEvolve updates the island archive, improvement estimates, bandit rewards, visit counts, migration metadata, and the currently active tactic. In TES terms, AdaEvolve uses ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) records not only to identify good candidates, but also to adapt the controller that allocates future budget across islands and search modes. This makes it a highly adaptive policy π \pi , but also one whose behavior depends on multiple interacting heuristics beyond evaluation scaling alone.

##### EvoX [ 82 ] .

EvoX operates on two timescales. The inner loop is a standard solution-discovery TES process: under an active strategy s t s_{t} , the policy constructs proposals by choosing parents, inspiration sets, and variation operators such as local refinement, free-form change, or structural divergence. The outer loop treats the strategy itself as an evolvable object. When progress over a sliding window falls below a stagnation threshold, a strategy-generator LLM receives the current population descriptor, prior strategies and their measured performance, a high-performing parent strategy, and inspirational strategies that worked in similar population states. It then proposes a new controller strategy. After each monitoring window, EvoX scores the deployed strategy, appends it to a strategy database, and may replace the active strategy without resetting the solution population. Under the TES framework, EvoX is notable because the optimized artifact is not only a solution y y , but also part of the policy π \pi that determines future proposal construction and archive updates. It therefore extends evaluation-driven scaling from solution search to controller search.

##### GEPA [ 2 ] .

GEPA is usually presented as prompt optimization for compound AI systems, but it also fits the TES template when the optimized artifact y y is a textual module, prompt, or agent scaffold. Each iteration selects a candidate from a Pareto frontier over per-example performance and then launches either a reflective mutation step or a merge step. To construct the next proposal, GEPA executes the selected candidate on a sampled minibatch, collects execution traces and evaluator-produced textual feedback, chooses which module to revise, and asks a reflection LLM to attribute successes and failures to specific prompt elements. A new candidate is admitted only if it improves on the minibatch; successful candidates are then evaluated more broadly and used to update the Pareto frontier. Thus, GEPA replaces parameter updates with textual reflection and Pareto-structured memory over prompt variants. In TES notation, its Φ \Phi is a reflection-based compressor from execution traces and feedback metadata m m into revised textual instructions. Compared with code-evolution systems, GEPA highlights that the same evaluation-driven loop applies beyond program synthesis, as long as candidate artifacts can be queried by an evaluator V V .

##### Summary.

Across these systems, evaluator feedback is the central information channel through which discovery improves. However, prior work often combines evaluation scaling with many other sources of improvement: ensemble generators, hand-designed prompt templates, archive heuristics, novelty filters, bandit controllers, meta-guidance, strategy evolution, or online model training. Our formulation abstracts these systems as policies π \pi that repeatedly construct proposals x n x_{n} from historical records ( y , r , m ) (y,r,m) and spend evaluator queries through V V . This abstraction motivates the simpler design studied in SimpleTES : fix the generator G G , make the evaluator budget N N explicit, and organize feedback-driven search through the compact dimensions ( C , L , K , Φ ) (C,L,K,\Phi) . Doing so does not subsume the full engineering richness of prior systems, but it provides a clean interface for studying what evaluation-driven scaling contributes by itself.

#### B.2 Self-evolving AI

The transition from static Large Language Models (LLMs) to self-evolving AI systems marks a paradigm shift toward Artificial Super Intelligence (ASI), where systems autonomously adapt their internal states, parameters, or architectural topologies based on interaction history and feedback. Following [ 40 ] , we summarize the methodology to develop self-evolving agents into 3 categories: reward-based self-evolution, imitation & demonstration learning, and evolutionary methods.

##### Reward-based Self-Evolution.

This line of research centers on closing the feedback loop through various reward signals to guide iterative improvement. Early frameworks like Reflexion [ 122 ] and AdaPlanner [ 135 ] leverage Textual Feedback , where the model generates natural language critiques to refine its future reasoning and memory. To reduce reliance on external supervision, Internal Reward mechanisms exploit the model’s own probability estimates or certainty to calibrate outputs [ 139 , 159 ] . Furthermore, External Rewards derived from sources outside the model, such as the environment [ 31 , 112 ] , majority voting [ 119 ] , or explicit rules [ 152 , 153 ] can also serve as an important signal for evolution.

##### Imitation & Demonstration Learning.

Stabilizing evolution by mimicking high-quality exemplars, imitation learning provides a prescriptive path to capability enhancement. This paradigm has evolved from human-centric demonstrations to Self-Generated Demonstrations , where agents like STaR [ 167 ] and Quiet-STaR [ 166 ] bootstrap their reasoning by fine-tuning on self-produced successful trajectories. Recent advancements also explore Cross-Agent Demonstration , enabling knowledge transfer within multi-agent systems where agents learn from the collective "experience library" of more capable peers [ 172 , 138 ] .

##### Population-based & Evolutionary Methods.

This paradigm focuses on agent improvement through evolutionary operators or iterative self-confrontation. Learning from evolution applies genetic operators—selection, mutation, and crossover—to discover improved capabilities in code, architecture, or parameters, exemplified by AlphaEvolve [ 100 ] . Alternatively, Self-Play creates a dynamic learning process where agents improve by interacting with versions of themselves, such as Absolute Zero [ 171 ] and R-Zero [ 57 ] .

In summary, these evolutionary paradigms collectively enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by strategically allocating additional computation and adapting to feedback during either training or inference. Building upon these foundations, SimpleTES extends the Learning from Evolution paradigm by leveraging explicit evaluator feedback to autonomously break through the performance upper bounds of difficult, open-ended scientific discovery problems without requiring any expert demonstrations. Furthermore, our training process aligns with the Self-Generated Demonstration approach by treating high-quality, long-horizon TES trajectories as a natural form of supervision, enabling the model to internalize global exploration strategies from its own trial-and-error experiences.

#### B.3 LLM for Scientific Discovery

The application of LLMs in scientific discovery is undergoing a fundamental paradigm shift, transitioning from passive assistance tools to autonomous research agents capable of end-to-end scientific investigation. Modern AI systems have moved towards closed-loop frameworks that independently propose hypotheses, design and execute experiments, and perform automated reviews. This rapid progress is driven by the scaling of search compute, the development of end-to-end autonomous systems, and the establishment of rigorous scientific benchmarks.

##### LLM based scientific discovery systems.

Several pioneering systems have demonstrated the potential for automating various stages of AI research. The AI Scientist [ 85 ] provides a pipeline for the research lifecycle, including phases for ideation, manuscript generation, and automated peer review. Other platforms, such as the Fully Automated Research System ( FARS ) [ 4 ] , utilize multi-agent topologies to generate numerous research artifacts in a fully automated manner. Additionally, the AutoResearch framework [ 68 ] implements an execution loop where agents autonomously modify code and validate performance improvements in real-time.

##### Scientific benchmarks for LLMs.

As LLM capabilities extend into complex engineering and reasoning, a new generation of benchmarks has emerged to evaluate their scientific proficiency. MLE-Bench [ 25 ] and PaperBench [ 134 ] assess the system’s capacity for high-level machine learning engineering and research reproducibility. In terms of performance optimization, KernelBench [ 103 ] targets systems-level GPU kernel design, while AlgoTune [ 108 ] focuses on accelerating algorithm execution in wall-clock time. Additionally, specialized tasks have been introduced to evaluate domain-specific expertise, such as symbolic regression [ 124 ] , quantum circuit design [ 160 ] , medical diagnostic reasoning [ 177 ] , and comprehensive physical research capabilities [ 94 ] .

##### Important results proposed by LLM systems.

Concurrent with the development of research systems and benchmarks, several significant breakthroughs have been achieved in fundamental sciences through LLM-driven methodologies. Using the AlphaEvolve [ 100 ] framework, researchers have conducted large-scale mathematical exploration, providing insights into complex problems such as functional inequalities and conjectures [ 42 ] . Google DeepMind has reported progress with AlphaProof [ 58 ] , which reached competitive standards in mathematical olympiads by combining LLMs with formal verification. Other developments include AlphaGeometry [ 145 ] for automated geometry solving and FunSearch [ 113 ] , which has been used to discover new solutions for combinatorial problems and surpass certain human-designed heuristics in algorithm optimization.

In summary, these systems and benchmarks illustrate a trajectory where AI is evolving from a research assistant into a self-directed collaborator. Building upon this foundation, our proposed SimpleTES framework advances this evolution by formalizing Test-Time Evaluation-driven Scaling (TES) to achieve generalizable breakthroughs in open-ended scientific problems without expert demonstrations.

#### B.4 Test-Time Scaling

Researchers have increasingly shifted their focus toward eliciting stronger intelligence during inference due to the gradually diminishing gains from scaling pretraining data and parameters. Inspired by cognitive science theories wherein complex problems trigger deeper, deliberate "System 2" thinking, Test-Time Scaling (TTS)—also referred to as test-time computing—allocates additional computation during inference to boost task performance and problem-solving capabilities. Following the unified hierarchical framework [ 170 ] , we can categorize TTS methods based on "what to scale" into four distinct paradigms: Parallel Scaling, Sequential Scaling, Hybrid Scaling, and Internal Scaling.

##### Parallel Scaling.

While LLMs traditionally generate a single response per query, parallel scaling enhances test-time performance by generating multiple candidate outputs concurrently and aggregating them into a final answer. A prominent line of research within this paradigm centers on the concept of Self-Consistency, which leverages sampling a diverse set of reasoning paths and applying majority voting to reduce generation variance and mitigate hallucinations [ 154 , 19 , 130 ] . Other approaches leverage multi-agent frameworks [ 64 ] or structured generation strategies [ 150 ] to further broaden the coverage and diversity of potential solutions.

##### Sequential Scaling.

In contrast to parallel generation, sequential scaling explicitly directs later computations based on intermediate steps, updating partial solution states iteratively. Since many complex tasks require deep deliberation rather than immediate pattern matching, this paradigm mimics a step-by-step refinement process. Notable implementations include Self-Refine [ 90 ] , which allows the model to iteratively critique and improve its own initial drafts without external training data. Other prominent works include ReAct [ 162 ] , which scales the capability of LLMs by sequentially interleaving reasoning and acting.

##### Hybrid Scaling.

Hybrid scaling exploits the complementary benefits of both parallel and sequential approaches. By generating multiple hypotheses in parallel (divergent thinking) and sequentially filtering or refining them (convergent thinking), hybrid methods can deeply explore promising reasoning paths while mitigating the risk of missing the correct answer. The Tree of Thoughts (ToT) framework [ 161 ] is a quintessential example, allowing the model to branch out at decision points and prune unpromising paths. This concept has been extensively expanded upon by Graph of Thoughts [ 12 ] and various implementations of Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) [ 36 , 141 ] .

##### Internal Scaling.

Internal scaling represents a recent paradigm shift wherein the model autonomously determines how much test-time computation to allocate without relying on external, human-guided prompting architectures. Through specific training procedures, these models learn internal policies that dictate when to continue reasoning and when to halt. Prime examples of this autonomous scaling include OpenAI’s o1 and o3 models [ 63 ] and DeepSeek-R1 [ 51 ] .

In summary, these four paradigms collectively enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by strategically allocating additional computation during inference. Building upon this foundation, SimpleTES further extends the conventional TTS paradigm to Test-Time Evaluation-driven Scaling (TES), relying on explicit evaluator feedback to enable the resolution of more difficult, open-ended scientific discovery problems.

### C Theoretical Modeling of SimpleTES

This section gives theoretical insights on the design choice of Definition 0.1 . Specifically, we will start by modeling the fundamental limitation of sequential refinement policy ( 1 , L , 1 , Φ ) (1,L,1,\Phi) , and then justify the necessity of global width C C by a theorem, and then explain the importance of local sample size K K . Since the goal is to understand the scaling effect of these dimensions, throughout the section we use a simplified mathematical model based on the Pólya Urn model [ 91 ] and do not consider the complexity induced by Φ \Phi . Readers might treat this section as a theoretical rewrite of SimpleTES in Methods.

A standard pure sequential refinement iteratively improves a solution conditioned on experiences from all previous candidates and evaluation results. Arguably, even equipped with an idealized generator with perfect attention and an infinite context window, this policy remains sub-optimal due to a fatal mismatch with the nature of open-ended problems. On one hand, solving a complex open-ended problem requires multidimensional coverage: a high-quality response must simultaneously satisfy multiple critical features to achieve a high evaluation score. On the other hand, refinements are path-dependent: the improvement space of subsequent solutions is largely determined by the direction of early-stage attempts. Although this is perhaps the nature of LLM-based generators, whose output depends primarily on historical context, it inevitably creates a “Matthew Effect” along the refinement trajectory, as early progress in one dimension attracts further refinement to that same dimension, starving other dimensions and trapping the search around local optima.

This empirical evidence clearly demonstrates the importance of identifying the correct factor for evaluation scaling.

Having demonstrated how compute should be allocated across multiple refinement trajectories, we turn to the scaling within each trajectory, with a given budget L = N / C L=N/C . At first glance, more refinement steps seem preferable, as each step can leverage feedback from previous attempts. However, this intuition could break down when path dependence is strong ( β \beta is large), when each refinement step further commits the trajectory to its current direction, making it harder to escape suboptimal regions.

##### Local sample size K K .

The preceding analysis motivates a second budget axis: the local sample size K K . While the number of independent trajectories C C mitigates global path dependence by exploring different refinement paths, K K addresses a more local failure mode: an individual refinement proposal may fail to improve the current solution. We model this by assuming that, after a dimension is selected according to the Pólya refinement rule in Definition .2 , the generator produces K K independent refinement proposals for that dimension. Each proposal improves the selected dimension with probability p p , and otherwise leaves it unchanged. The best proposal is then applied. Thus, increasing K K raises the probability that a refinement step results in an actual improvement from p p to 1 − ( 1 − p ) K 1-(1-p)^{K} , but it also reduces the number of sequential refinement steps under a fixed total budget.

We empirically illustrate this trade-off in Figure 1 . We simulate problems parameterized by ( D , λ , β ) (D,\lambda,\beta) under the refinement model in Definition .2 , fixing L = 4096 L=4096 , β = 4 \beta=4 , and C = 32 C=32 independent trajectories, and report the normalized final bottleneck score averaged over 2048 simulations. The results show that moderate values of K K substantially improve performance over purely sequential refinement ( K = 1 ) (K=1) , especially when the improvement probability p p is large enough for local sampling to reliably find a useful proposal. However, very large K K can degrade performance, since allocating more budget to local sampling leaves fewer refinement steps. This confirms that K K should be treated as a trade-off parameter rather than a monotonic source of improvement. We further study this interaction between C C , L L , and K K on real open-ended tasks in Section F .

Together, the total evaluation budget N = C × K × L N=C\times K\times L has been decomposed into three scaling dimensions: global independent trajectories C C , local sample size K K , and the actual refinement depth L = N C ​ K L=\frac{N}{CK} . In short, this section aims to recognize the simple yet effective factors to be scaled, with the evidence below showing that by wisely scaling these parameters, we can achieve state-of-the-art solutions on most problems considered. Arguably, there is clear room for further improvements. For instance, the current budget is divided evenly across each trajectory. Clearly, discarding unsatisfying trajectories on-the-fly and saving budgets for promising trajectories could be helpful. Additionally, one might recognize the fractal structure of C C and L L , both being i.i.d. attempts at different levels. As an analogy, breaking L L sequential refinement steps into smaller segments to couple more complexity might be beneficial. Relevant ablations can be found in Section F .

#### D.3 From Golden Metrics to Surrogate: Hacking Analysis

As formalized in Methods, the paradigm of TES relies on an explicit, queryable surrogate evaluator V V to approximate the true, often inaccessible, golden metric. However, the purely score-driven nature of feedback-driven search inevitably drives models to discover and exploit the discrepancies between the surrogate and the golden metric. This results in various forms of reward hacking, where the model optimizes for the surrogate’s implementation vulnerabilities rather than the underlying task. We systematically categorize these phenomena below.

##### Hacking in math construction tasks.

In mathematical construction tasks, the surrogate evaluator is designed to be an exact metric (e.g., a formal geometric verifier). However, the gap here emerges from implementation vulnerabilities in numerical processing .

The first hacking strategy targets the evaluator’s decision tolerance. In the Circle Packing in a Unit Square task, the model is required to place several circles in a unit square without overlapping. However, the surrogate evaluator provided by OpenEvolve [ 6 ] employs a floating-point tolerance of 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} for overlap determination. The model fully exploits this proxy limitation by crafting configurations where circles and boundaries actually overlap, but the overlapping magnitude strictly remains under the 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} threshold. See for the construction.

The second hacking strategy exploits floating-point precision. For instance, evaluators used by EvoX [ 82 ] compute metrics directly on raw coordinates without proper normalization steps. The model exploits this vulnerability by providing degenerate configurations—such as collapsing all points to an infinitesimal scale ( ∼ 10 − 162 \sim 10^{-162} ) or arranging them collinearly—which induce severe numerical instability in the surrogate, yielding artificially high scores.

##### Hacking in kernel optimization tasks.

In kernel optimization tasks, the gap between the surrogate evaluator and the golden metric is fundamentally wider. Rather than relying on a mathematically rigid verifier, the surrogate here acts as a vulnerable empirical estimator forced to operate within a highly complex execution environment. Consequently, models escalate from simple numerical exploits to sophisticated manipulations designed to bypass the benchmarking process entirely. We identify four primary hacking strategies:

1. Caching results over multiple runs : To ensure accurate latency measurements and reduce variance, evaluators often repeatedly call the target kernel using the exact same inputs. ##### Discussion.

Taken together, these phenomena demonstrate that, despite being entirely blind to the evaluator’s underlying source code, LLMs exhibit a remarkable capability to autonomously discover and exploit surrogate gaps. By corrupting the evaluation feedback loop, these behaviors mislead the optimization process into yielding deceptive solutions rather than genuinely advancing target capabilities. Currently, closing this gap between the proxy and reality relies heavily on human-in-the-loop interventions, requiring multiple rounds of manual debugging and iterative patching of evaluator loopholes. Developing fully automated, robust evaluation frameworks capable of aligning the proxy metric with the ultimate objective—while dynamically detecting such logical hacking behaviors—remains a critical open challenge for future work.

### E Variants of Proposal Constructor Φ \Phi

##### Balance policy.

The Balance policy is a stratified random sampling strategy designed to balance the exploitation of high-performing solutions with the exploration of diverse, sub-optimal trajectories. Let S S be the set of historical attempts within a trajectory, sorted in descending order of their evaluator scores such that s 1 ≥ s 2 ≥ ⋯ ≥ s | S | s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\dots\geq s_{|S|} . To construct a proposal with n n inspirations, the policy enforces that the absolute best historical solution, s 1 s_{1} , is always deterministically included.

For the remaining n − 1 n-1 slots, the policy categorizes the sorted historical trials into three overlapping tiers: an exploitation tier T exploit T_{\text{exploit}} containing the top r elite r_{\text{elite}} fraction of attempts, an exploration tier T explore T_{\text{explore}} containing mid-tier solutions (typically between the 10th and 60th percentiles), and a global random tier T random = S T_{\text{random}}=S covering all prior attempts. Each subsequent inspiration is sampled without replacement according to the following probability distribution: Tier ∼ { T exploit with probability ​ p exploit , T explore with probability ​ p explore , T random with probability ​ 1 − p exploit − p explore . \displaystyle\text{Tier}\sim\begin{cases}T_{\text{exploit}}&\text{with probability }p_{\text{exploit}},\\ T_{\text{explore}}&\text{with probability }p_{\text{explore}},\\ T_{\text{random}}&\text{with probability }1-p_{\text{exploit}}-p_{\text{explore}}.\end{cases} (23) Once a tier is selected, an attempt is drawn uniformly at random from that tier. This heuristic ensures that the prompt is primarily grounded in elite solutions while consistently injecting varied structural contexts to prevent premature convergence.

##### LLM-Elite policy.

While score-based heuristics like the Balance policy are efficient, evaluating a trial’s potential solely by its scalar score can be myopic. The LLM-Elite policy addresses this by leveraging semantic insights to dynamically maintain a bounded-size "elite pool" 𝒫 \mathcal{P} (with maximum capacity L elite L_{\text{elite}} ) that maximizes both solution quality and methodological diversity.

Whenever a new candidate solution x new x_{\text{new}} is generated and evaluated, an auxiliary LLM acts as a gatekeeper. The LLM is provided with the scores and self-reflective summaries (detailing the approach and insights) of both x new x_{\text{new}} and all current solutions in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} . It is instructed to output one of three actions: Add , Replace ( j ) (j) (to swap out a redundant or inferior attempt j ∈ 𝒫 j\in\mathcal{P} ), or Reject . To prevent the LLM from inadvertently discarding substantial progress due to misjudging diversity, we enforce a strict monotonic override rule: if the score of x new x_{\text{new}} is strictly greater than the maximum score currently in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} , it bypasses the LLM’s rejection and is deterministically added (replacing the lowest-scoring solution if | 𝒫 | = L elite |\mathcal{P}|=L_{\text{elite}} ).

To sample n n inspirations from 𝒫 \mathcal{P} for a new proposal, we employ the identical stratified random sampling mechanism described in the Balance policy. The attempts in 𝒫 \mathcal{P} are sorted in descending order by their scores and divided into the exploitation, exploration, and global random tiers. Trials are then sampled without replacement according to the previously defined tier probabilities. This approach heavily favors the inclusion of the highest-scoring elites while retaining a non-zero probability to draw inspiration from lower-ranked, yet semantically distinct, solutions curated by the LLM. Furthermore, this policy injects a concise text-only overview of the entire elite pool into the prompt (excluding raw code), allowing the generator to comprehend the global landscape of explored directions without consuming an excessive context window.

#### F.2 Ablation on Reflection and Failure Patterns

Recall that our algorithm constructs the next query by integrating the task instruction, the selected inspirations S ( c ) S^{(c)} , and the chain-local memory ℛ ( c ) \mathcal{R}^{(c)} . To isolate and evaluate the individual contributions of these two mechanisms, we conduct an ablation study on the First Autocorrelation Inequality and the Erdős Minimum-Overlap Problem. We systematically test four configurations: disabling both features, enabling only Reflection, enabling only Failure Patterns, and enabling both. The results demonstrate that incorporating explicit negative constraints (Failure Patterns) provides a consistently strong foundation. #### F.3 Efficiency Analysis: Trajectory-level Pruning

While scaling the number of parallel chains generally improves search outcomes, the vast search space often leads to many chains exploring suboptimal directions. To effectively scale our search process and prevent the wasting of computational budget on unpromising trajectories, we introduce pruning strategies. Pruning acts as an early-stopping mechanism: by identifying and terminating underperforming chains during the generation process, we can reallocate computational resources toward the most promising directions without significantly compromising the final solution quality.

To systematically evaluate the impact of pruning, we conduct experiments across six mathematical tasks: three autocorrelation tasks, two circle packing tasks, and the Erdős Minimum-Overlap Problem. Each task is evaluated 3 times with a total chain length of L = 100 L=100 and number of chains C = 32 C=32 (Fix K = 16 K=16 ). We examine pruning through two complementary settings: a two-stage sweep with nominal cutoffs at L = 25 L=25 and L = 50 L=50 , summarized in Figure 7 , and a task-level single-cutoff ablation at nominal L = 12 L=12 and L = 25 L=25 , reported in Table 4 . At each designated cutoff, active chains are ranked by the best score attained up to that point, and a predefined proportion of the lowest-scoring chains is eliminated.

The resulting trade-off, summarized in Figure 7 , demonstrates a surprisingly high preservation rate for the best final score under aggressive pruning. For instance, even when applying a stringent first-stage cutoff at the nominal L = 25 L=25 checkpoint that retains only a single chain, the original best final score is preserved in 10 out of the 18 total runs. This suggests that for a substantial portion of successful trials, the structural advantages of the final solution manifest early in the search process. Furthermore, across the two-stage configurations shown in Figure 7 , the relative degradation remains remarkably minimal. In the majority of configurations, the expected score degradation is bounded within 0.01 % 0.01\% , and all configurations exhibit degradation below 0.03 % 0.03\% .

The task-level single-cutoff results in Table 4 further clarify the task dependence underlying the aggregate trend. For the circle packing tasks, early pruning exerts virtually no negative impact, as the performance upper bound is easily accessible and a large proportion of chains can successfully reach it. Conversely, for tasks such as the Autocorrelation Inequalities and the Erdős Minimum-Overlap Problem, initial performance is not always a reliable proxy for final solution quality. In these scenarios, chains often require extended iterative refinement before their true potential is realized. Consequently, the earlier and more aggressive single-cutoff settings can lead to higher elimination rates of optimal solutions and more noticeable score degradation.

These findings affirm that while pruning is an effective and necessary technique for scaling parallel search, its implementation presents multiple challenges. The primary difficulty lies in adaptively selecting a pruning strategy that aligns with the task’s specific landscape. Furthermore, relying solely on intermediate scalar scores for elimination can be myopic. Future work will focus on developing more sophisticated pruning algorithms that incorporate richer contextual signals—such as textual reasoning patterns, trajectory growth trends, and structural heuristics—to make more informed early-stopping decisions.

### H Quantum Compilation Tasks

This section gives supporting details for the quantum compilation tasks. We separate the material into four parts: problem definitions for the two hardware settings, experimental scaffolds and evaluation setup, discovered programs for the main SimpleTES results, and an additional superconducting routing policy obtained by SimpleTES using Gemini.

#### H.1 Problem definitions

##### Superconducting qubit routing.

On superconducting quantum computers, this compilation task is typically called qubit routing , and is often discussed together with qubit mapping or qubit allocation [ 128 , 28 ] . Superconducting quantum computers encode qubits in electrical circuits fabricated on a chip, typically using Josephson-junction-based devices operated at cryogenic temperatures [ 70 ] . Because these qubits occupy fixed chip locations, native two-qubit operations are available only between selected pairs of physical qubits. This hardware connectivity is represented by a sparse coupling graph, such as a grid lattice or a heavy-hex layout, whose low degree helps reduce frequency collisions and crosstalk while remaining scalable ( Figure 2 a).

This objective is motivated by the fact that SWAPs add extra two-qubit operations and circuit depth; in the evaluation convention used here, one SWAP is decomposed into three added CNOTs [ 76 ] . From a complexity perspective, qubit routing is closely related to token swapping and graph reconfiguration and is NP-hard in general [ 128 , 61 ] . A routing decision that helps one gate may also change the placement seen by all later gates, which makes the long-horizon component essential.

##### Zoned neutral-atom compilation.

Neutral-atom platforms store qubits in atoms and use Rydberg interactions to perform entangling gates. A two-qubit gate can be applied only after the participating atoms are brought within the Rydberg interaction radius [ 133 , 137 ] . In a zoned architecture, the static SLM (Spatial Light Modulator) traps are divided into a storage zone and an entangling zone: idle atoms remain in storage, while atoms participating in the current two-qubit layer are moved into the entangling zone [ 15 , 133 , 80 ] . This separation avoids unnecessarily exposing atoms outside the current interaction layer to a global Rydberg pulse.

Atom transport is implemented by AODs (Acousto-Optic Deflectors), which consist of independently controlled horizontal and vertical tweezer lines. Each intersection between an AOD row and an AOD column creates a potential movable trap. Open activates new AOD rows or columns and loads atoms into AOD traps when these newly activated lines create intersections that coincide with occupied atomic sites. Close deactivates selected AOD rows or columns and releases atoms currently carried on those lines back to the corresponding SLM sites. Move translates the currently activated AOD lines while carrying all atoms loaded on the AOD.

Within one rearrangement step, these operations are subject to two main shuttling constraints. The non-crossing constraint requires active parallel AOD rows or columns to remain separated by at least 2 ​ μ ​ m 2\,\mu\mathrm{m} , so distinct active AOD lines can neither merge into the same position nor exchange their relative order [ 133 , 132 ] . The ghost-spot constraint arises because activating multiple AOD rows and columns simultaneously creates all pairwise row–column intersections as live traps, so a legal rearrangement must avoid unintended auxiliary traps on occupied sites [ 133 , 132 ] ( Figure 2 d).

Zoned neutral-atom compilation involves challenging, tightly coupled decisions about atom placement and AOD-constrained routing. Their effects extend across stages: a placement that shortens one transition can still serialize the next once AOD constraints are enforced, and a reuse decision can save transfers in the current stage while forcing longer motion later. Candidate placements must therefore be evaluated over the full placement trajectory rather than one stage at a time [ 133 , 80 , 132 , 131 ] .

#### H.2 Experimental scaffolds and evaluation setup

##### Superconducting routing setup.

Our experimental scaffold fixes a complete routing engine (circuit parsing, dependency tracking, legality checks, and output construction) and exposes only the decision policy . Concretely, the mutable surface contains two hooks: (i) initial layout construction (a mapping from logical to physical qubits) and (ii) online SWAP selection (choosing a legal SWAP edge at each routing step). This design maximizes the algorithmic search space, allowing the model to implement a broad range of routing strategies. At the same time, it cleanly separates SWAP selection from gate execution, guaranteeing correctness by preventing the model from exploiting invalid gate operations.

Initial program. The initial policy is a refactor of Qiskit’s released LightSABRE Rust implementation, embedded inside the fixed engine. The initial layout procedure is simplified to encourage diverse exploration, while the routing policy stays effectively the same as the LightSABRE regime. This baseline provides a strong hand-engineered starting point with a well-studied quality–runtime tradeoff.

Evaluation. Candidate policies are compiled and executed on a benchmark suite. Routing cost is measured by the number of inserted SWAPs. We evaluate on the 24 circuits selected from the original SABRE study [ 76 ] , across three coupling graphs: IBM’s 20-qubit Q20 , Google’s 105-qubit Willow processor [ 47 ] , and IBM’s 156-qubit heavy-hex architecture Heron [ 97 , 59 ] , yielding 72 routing instances in total. The baselines are SABRE [ 76 ] and its modern variant LightSABRE [ 176 ] . For LightSABRE, we choose the configuration of swap_trials=20, layout_trials=20, max_iterations=4 from the original paper.

##### Zoned neutral-atom setup.

Our scaffold exposes the compiler as executable code inside a fixed framework that defines the interfaces, solver, and evaluator. The compilation pipeline has four components: a scheduler that partitions a logical ZNAACircuit into one-qubit and two-qubit stages, a reuse analyzer that marks qubits reused across adjacent two-qubit stages, a placer that outputs full placement snapshots, and a router that converts consecutive placements into legal Open , Move , and Close operations. A fixed solver invokes these components in order, and the fixed evaluator replays the emitted operations on a ZNAAMachine , and rejects invalid compilations. In our setting, the editable evolve block modifies only the placer ; the scheduler, reuse analyzer, and router remain fixed.

Initial program. The initial program exposes the editable evolve block while keeping the surrounding pipeline fixed. In Round 1, that block contained a trivial placeholder placer. Each later round initialized the editable block from the strongest placer discovered in the previous round.

Evaluation. We evaluate candidate placers on a 36-circuit suite built from QASMBench [ 75 ] circuits and MQT Bench [ 110 ] generated circuits, spanning textbook quantum algorithms, state preparation, variational circuits, quantum simulation, quantum machine learning, and arithmetic circuits, with sizes from 6 to 500 qubits. The fixed evaluator executes each compiled plan, records correctness, total execution time, and fidelity-related losses from gate execution, transport, transfers, and decoherence, and scores each circuit against a cached baseline from the fixed scaffold. Our main comparison baseline is the reuse-aware ZAC-style compiler [ 80 ] .

#### H.3 Discovered programs

##### Discovered superconducting algorithm.

The discovered algorithm can be summarized as follows. First, the discovered algorithm invests heavily in initial layout : it seeds high-degree logical qubits onto central, high-degree physical qubits, and then refines the mapping through an aggressive stack of hill-climbing and restart-based local search. Second, it strengthens online SWAP selection : it broadens the candidate neighborhood beyond front-layer incident edges to include look-ahead and shortest-path edges, changes the look-ahead term into dynamic horizon with exponential decay, and reshapes the swap objective to explicitly reward immediate gate executability. Together, these changes preserve the overall LightSABRE-style structure while materially improving robustness against long-range interactions and stagnation.

##### Discovered zoned neutral-atom algorithm.

The discovered placer replaces stage-local travel minimization with whole-trajectory routing-aware search, so it explicitly optimizes the parallelism that survives under the fixed router rather than only local travel distance. It begins by constructing a moderate set of heuristic initial storage layouts, including interaction-graph-based qubit orderings, their reversed counterparts, and a few shuffled orders. From each initial layout, it runs a full forward placement pass to build a complete placement trajectory, and then compares these candidates using router-aware evaluation.

Within a single forward pass, active qubits are placed stage by stage. When a stage loads qubits from storage into the entangling zone, a qubit whose partner is already reused is assigned to the complementary column of the same entangling site, whereas the remaining two-qubit pairs are assigned by Hungarian matching, with physical Euclidean distance as the placement cost. When active non-reused qubits return from the entangling zone to storage, they are grouped by source entangling row and ordered by source column. A monotone dynamic program then selects destination storage cells that preserve this order while minimizing column deviation, after which an intra-row swap refinement is applied whenever it shortens travel distance and remains compatible with the router’s non-crossing constraint.

After a complete trajectory has been constructed, the algorithm evaluates the full placement sequence with the fixed router. Candidates are ranked first by the number of router-emitted Move operations and then, for ties, by total Euclidean travel distance. Starting from the best candidate, the algorithm performs several rounds of reverse-through-time refinement, in which a reverse pass seeded by the current final layout is fed back into a new forward pass, and then applies hill-climbing swaps on the initial storage layout. An update is accepted only when it reduces the number of router-emitted Move operations or preserves that count while improving travel distance.

#### H.4 Discovered superconducting routing policy using Gemini

The superconducting qubit routing experiment was additionally run with gemini-3-pro-preview . This run produced a policy family in which both the initial placement and the online routing rule are explicitly shaped by circuit structure and hardware geometry. For initial layout, the discovered program classifies logical interaction components by morphology, such as path-like, ring-like, or dense structure. It then selects physical regions with matching shape and connectivity, prioritizes central and well-connected physical qubits for high-traffic logical qubits, and refines the mapping through large-scale randomized restarts and local improvement.

For online routing, the program strengthens SWAP selection with criticality-aware scoring. Front-layer and look-ahead gates are weighted by reverse depth and critical-path relevance, while stronger decay and centrality-sensitive preferences steer active qubits toward more useful regions of the device and help avoid stagnation. Quantitatively, the discovered program reduces added SWAPs by 24.7 % 24.7\% relative to SABRE and 18.3 % 18.3\% relative to LightSABRE. The largest gains occur on Q20 , where the reductions in added SWAPs reach 40.7 % 40.7\% and 32.9 % 32.9\% , respectively. Table 5 gives the topology-level breakdown. These results suggest that effective qubit routing benefits from a more explicitly graph-theoretic treatment, in which circuit morphology, interaction criticality, and hardware connectivity are exploited jointly.

#### I.5 How evolution changes mission-design search

To examine the search procedure, we replayed the archived initial and evolved programs with lightweight instrumentation. ### K Scaling Law Discovery

##### Overview.

Scaling law discovery [ 79 , 78 ] studies how machine learning performance changes with scale and aims to identify compact symbolic laws that extrapolate from small-scale experiments to larger regimes. In foundation-model development, such laws are used to predict quantities such as training loss, downstream error, or task-specific metrics from variables including model size, dataset size, vocabulary size, learning rate, batch size, and architectural choices. The central challenge is not merely to fit a curve, but to recover a concise analytic form whose structure is not known a priori and that generalizes across related experimental settings. Each trial consists of a set of input variables, a target quantity, and a control index identifying the experimental context, such as a model family, dataset, or domain. The control index makes it possible to search for a shared symbolic form across settings while allowing the coefficients to vary within each setting. This makes scaling law discovery especially suitable for SimpleTES : candidate laws can be represented as executable programs, evaluated automatically on held-out extrapolation performance, and compared across a large, structured design space involving symbolic form, asymptotic behavior, parameter sharing, and fitting procedures. Compared with standard regression, the emphasis here is on symbolic structure and extrapolation; compared with broader agentic ML benchmarks, the objective is not to engineer an end-to-end workflow, but to discover a compact law that transfers across settings and remains accurate in unseen scaling regimes. We formalize scaling law discovery as follows.

A canonical example is pretraining scaling, where the inputs are model size N N and dataset size D D , the target is training loss L L , and the objective is to discover a law of the form L ≈ f θ ​ ( N , D ) L\approx f_{\theta}(N,D) . The same framework also covers scaling with domain mixture, learning rate, batch size, and related variables. What distinguishes this task from ordinary regression is that success is measured primarily by extrapolation beyond the fitted regime, not by interpolation within it.

##### Experimental setting.

We report results on the four-task SLDBench subset: parallel (36 seen / 12 unseen) [ 26 ] , domain_mix (80 seen / 24 unseen) [ 163 ] , lr&bsz (2,702 seen / 117 unseen) [ 77 ] , and u_shape (389 seen / 127 unseen) [ 157 ] . Following the original SLDBench protocol, the unseen split is always constructed as an extrapolation test set by holding out the largest model sizes, compute regimes, or other extreme settings, rather than by using random interpolation-style splits. The execution environment also follows SLDBench: agents operate in a sandbox terminal with a minimal Python stack ( scikit-learn , pandas , and datasets ) and no network access, and must implement the discovered law and its parameter-fitting subroutine under the required function signature. Final performance is measured by test-set R 2 R^{2} , clipped to [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] , on the hidden extrapolation split, where higher is better. We set C = 16 C=16 , L = 20 L=20 , and K = 16 K=16 for SimpleTES on this task.

To ensure a controlled comparison, our method uses the same initialization, task instruction, and evaluator as SLDAgent. In particular, the initial program is the same baseline program pair used by SLDAgent: a naive power-law scaling_law_func together with a standard BFGS-based fit_scaling_law optimizer. We also adopt the same task-specific instruction template, which asks the model to evolve both the symbolic expression and the fitting routine from this baseline, while emphasizing extrapolation accuracy, cross-setting generalization, parameter efficiency, and numerical/theoretical stability; the instruction additionally provides task context, function signatures, and data characteristics such as feature definitions and value ranges. The evaluator is likewise kept identical to the SLDAgent setting. This alignment is important because SLDAgent is itself an evolution-based method built on top of the OpenEvolve framework, using iterative mutation and evaluation of candidate programs; accordingly, our comparison isolates the effect of the evolutionary strategy rather than differences in initialization, prompting, or evaluation pipeline.

### L Whole-brain neural-activity forecast on ZAPBench

##### Problem definition and benchmark design.

ZAPBench evaluates neural-activity forecasting at whole-brain, single-neuron resolution. Structural maps of neural wiring are essential for understanding circuit organization, but they do not by themselves specify the time-varying circuit dynamics that transform sensory input and internal state into neural responses. A functional account therefore requires predicting how activity evolves across the brain over time. ZAPBench provides a quantitative benchmark for this problem by asking models to forecast the activities of all recorded neurons from a short temporal context, enabling direct comparison of forecasting models and analysis of how whole-brain dynamics unfold across stimulus conditions. It is built from a two-hour light-sheet fluorescence recording of a larval zebrafish that was exposed to a sequence of visual stimuli designed to elicit a range of behaviors; the raw volumetric video was aligned, motion-stabilized, and segmented into activity traces, yielding a data matrix of 71,721 71{,}721 neurons over 7,879 7{,}879 time steps (a sampling interval of roughly 914 914 ms per step) spanning nine stimulus conditions ( gain , dots , flash , taxis , turning , position , open loop , rotation , and dark ).

##### Forecasting task and data splits.

The task is to predict the joint future activity of all 71,721 71{,}721 neurons up to 32 32 steps ahead given only 4 4 steps of past context—a high-dimensional multivariate forecasting problem. Because the traces are derived from the volumetric recording, a forecaster may operate either on the extracted per-neuron traces or on the 3D volumes directly: the benchmark’s strongest hand-designed baseline is a volumetric U-Net that predicts the brain video, exploiting spatial structure that is lost when the volumes are reduced to traces, but at a much higher computational cost—the video model is reported to train in roughly 36 36 hours on 16 16 A100 GPUs, versus under four hours on a single 16GB GPU for trace-based forecasters. Following ZAPBench, each stimulus condition is split 70 / 10 / 20 70/10/20 into contiguous train, validation, and test segments.

##### Evaluation and search protocol.

Candidate programs are scored by the mean absolute error (MAE) between predicted and recorded activity, averaged over all neurons and over the full 32 32 -step (or 1 1 , 4 4 , 8 8 , 16 16 steps in different tasks) horizon. The validation split serves two purposes: it drives model selection including hyperparameter choices and early stopping; and it supplies the single scalar (validation MAE) that guides the search. The test split is never seen during search or model selection. SimpleTES is initialized from one neural starter program, a shared-weight per-neuron forecaster (Fig. 4 f), and edits the body of its forecast routine. Each candidate is trained and scored in an isolated evaluator.

### M Single-Cell RNA-Seq Denoising

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) resolves gene expression in individual cells [ 89 , 173 , 87 ] , but its measurements are corrupted by low capture efficiency and stochastic dropout [ 146 ] , motivating dedicated denoising algorithms [ 147 ] . Denoising has no ground truth to check against; quality is assessed by the molecular cross-validation framework of [ 11 ] , which partitions observed UMI counts by binomial subsampling and underpins the OpenProblems benchmark [ 86 ] . The core difficulty is a tension between two objectives: mean-squared error in log-normalized space rewards faithful recovery of relative expression, while the Poisson negative log-likelihood rewards count consistency after library-size rescaling, and aggressively optimizing one degrades the other.

We follow the molecular cross-validation protocol [ 11 ] , splitting the Pancreas dataset into a training matrix the algorithm observes and a held-out test matrix it does not. Candidates are scored on two normalized metrics, log-space MSE and Poisson negative log-likelihood, with the Poisson term acting as a hard constraint that rejects any solution failing it, and the final score is the mean of the two. Following [ 165 ] , we initialize SimpleTES with MAGIC [ 147 ] , cap each candidate at 400 seconds, and run evolution exclusively on Pancreas; PBMC and Tabula Muris Senis Lung are withheld entirely and used only for final evaluation, so the reported numbers measure cross-dataset generalization to held-out datasets.

Across the held-out datasets in Table 15 , SimpleTES reaches a Tabula score of 0.74 0.74 , surpassing the prior state-of-the-art method TTT-Discover [ 165 ] ( 0.73 0.73 ) and matching it on PBMC ( 0.71 0.71 ), while outperforming MAGIC [ 147 ] , ALRA [ 81 ] , and every other baseline on both. Because evolution never saw PBMC or Tabula, these gains are direct evidence that the discovered algorithm generalizes rather than fitting the search distribution.

Inspection shows that SimpleTES departs structurally from the baselines. Where MAGIC builds a single diffusion operator and TTT-Discover elaborates that template with variance-stabilizing transform ensembling and low-rank refinement, the discovered program constructs several independent denoisers, including multi-scale diffusion under both correlation- and Euclidean-based graph operators, log-space diffusion, neighbor averaging, PCA imputation, and NMF reconstruction, and scores each internally on the training split alone. It then blends them into a data-driven ensemble, weighting candidates by their inverse Poisson and MSE losses under the Poisson constraint, and applies a gene-mean calibration that clips per-gene scaling near 1.0 1.0 to preserve count fidelity. None of these choices are dataset-specific: the ensemble weights are fit from the training split at runtime, which is what lets the same program generalize to the unseen PBMC and Tabula distributions.

### N Lasso Regularization Path

##### Overview.

The lasso regularization path is a core computational primitive in high-dimensional statistics, arising naturally in cross-validation and model selection across domains from genomics to finance. Computing the full path of solutions across a grid of regularization values is orders of magnitude more efficient than solving each problem independently, but demands careful algorithmic design to exploit warm starts and sparsity structure. The de facto standard solver, glmnet [ 39 ] , represents decades of expert engineering. This task asks whether SimpleTES can discover a solver that is faster than glmnet while maintaining the same float64 precision and correctness guarantees.

##### Experimental setting.

Each candidate is a self-contained C++ program that reads a binary-encoded problem from standard input and writes the complete coefficient path to standard output. Correctness is evaluated on a separate fresh problem that is not used for timing, preventing caching or test-set overfitting. The surrogate metric optimized during evolution is the geometric mean of solve times over 17 synthetic problem sizes. These instances span the axes that govern lasso-path performance: sample-to-feature ratio n / p n/p , design-matrix sparsity, active-set density, and feature correlation from near-independent to highly correlated Toeplitz structure.

This evaluation surface is deliberately heterogeneous: a solver specialized for wide problems with p ≫ n p\gg n can fail on tall problems with n ≫ p n\gg p , and vice versa. The 11 real-world datasets in Table 16 are not used during evolution and therefore test whether the discovered solver generalizes beyond the synthetic search distribution. We initialize SimpleTES from a faithful C++ port of glmnet ’s Gaussian lasso path, including the covariance update for p < 500 p<500 , the naive residual update for p ≥ 500 p\geq 500 , warm starts, sequential strong-rule screening [ 142 ] , active-set inner loops, and KKT verification.

##### Results analysis.

All reported solutions pass the held-out correctness check. Across the 11 real-world datasets, SimpleTES achieves an average speedup of 2.17 × 2.17\times over glmnet and 14.08 × 14.08\times over sklearn . The gains are largest in tall regimes: on DNA ( 1700 × 180 1700\times 180 ), SimpleTES is 9.56 × 9.56\times faster than glmnet . It also improves on the large sparse RCV1 design by 1.76 × 1.76\times over glmnet and 5.49 × 5.49\times over sklearn ; the gains against sklearn are particularly large on high-dimensional biological datasets, including 50.77 × 50.77\times on TCGA BRCA and 10.16 × 10.16\times on Duke Breast Cancer.

##### Case study.

The initialization uses coordinate descent throughout: the covariance method for p < 500 p<500 and the naive residual method for p ≥ 500 p\geq 500 . The discovered program instead uses a geometry-aware switching rule. For moderate-dimensional, tall designs ( p ≤ 2000 p\leq 2000 and n ≥ p / 4 n\geq p/4 ), it replaces coordinate descent with an exact LARS homotopy solver that traces the regularization path analytically and updates the active set at each kink with rank-one inverse-Gram updates [ 34 ] . For wide or sparse designs, it retains coordinate descent with strong-rule screening, an active-set inner loop, and KKT verification.

### O Mathematical Scientific Discovery Tasks

Extremal analysis and combinatorial construction ask for explicit mathematical solutions that optimize precisely defined quantities: a distribution, a witness function, an integer set, a geometric packing, or a sign matrix. These tasks are a natural setting for scientific discovery because progress depends on finding the right structural solution, and each candidate can be checked by a deterministic evaluator rather than by a noisy empirical proxy.

#### O.1 Extremal analysis

Extremal analysis tasks search over constrained functions and optimize overlap or autoconvolution functionals. Candidate solutions are represented as discretized step functions or signed piecewise-constant witnesses, with feasibility enforced by support, range, and unit-mass constraints.

##### Erdős minimum-overlap problem.

Erdős’ minimum-overlap problem asks how to distribute a unit amount of mass over [ 0 , 2 ] [0,2] so that the distribution has as little one-sided overlap as possible with the complements of its shifted copies.

The evaluator reports the translated-overlap objective through 1 / Ψ ⁡ ( h ) 1/\Psi(h) during search, while the standard value Ψ ⁡ ( h ) \Psi(h) is reported for comparison.

Best result and discovered solution. The discovered witness is near-binary: most grid cells are driven close to either zero or one, rather than remaining near the uniform initialization. This structure arranges the unit mass so that the translated-overlap profile is flattened across the high-risk shift range, reducing the worst shift rather than only improving the average shift behavior. The solution is therefore interpretable as an equioscillation-style witness: the best solution balances multiple competing translated-overlap constraints instead of optimizing a single shift locally ( Figure 10 a).

Case Analysis. The strongest program uses a coarse-to-fine optimization pipeline. It first searches over coarse discretizations to identify promising mass layouts, then refines them with constrained local optimization and projected polishing steps that preserve the exact discrete mass constraint and the box constraint 0 ≤ h ≤ 1 0\leq h\leq 1 . This code structure is important because naive local perturbations around the uniform witness tend to improve only a small subset of shifts, whereas coarse-to-fine refinement can reorganize the global mass pattern before polishing the final overlap profile.

##### Autocorrelation Inequalities.

The First, Second, and Third Autocorrelation Inequalities form a family of extremal autoconvolution problems.

During search, the evaluators cast the tasks as maximization by using 1 / Φ 1 1/\Phi_{1} , Φ 2 \Phi_{2} , and 1 / Φ 3 1/\Phi_{3} , while the standard objectives are reported in the result tables.

Best results and discovered solutions. The three autocorrelation tasks lead to different witness structures. For AC1, the strongest discovered witness concentrates mass near the support boundary, reducing the peak of the non-negative autoconvolution but still trailing the leading public result. For AC2, the best witness is sparse and produces a long near-flat plateau in the autoconvolution profile, a structure that improves the norm ratio by avoiding a narrow peak. For AC3, the signed witness becomes oscillatory and uses cancellation to control the largest absolute autoconvolution value. These solutions show that the same evaluation-driven loop can search across non-negative and signed witness classes without a task-specific symbolic derivation ( Figure 10 b–d).

Case Analysis. The best programs implement different search strategies for the three objectives. AC1 uses a simplex-projected mass-transfer search over a 1024-bin witness, repeatedly moving mass while preserving non-negativity and unit mass. AC2 combines FFT-based convolution evaluation with L-BFGS-B refinement, making it efficient to evaluate many candidate witnesses and polish sparse structures. AC3 uses a discrete cosine transform parameterization that directly searches signed oscillatory patterns and optimizes the cancellation structure of f ∗ f f\ast f . The code-level adaptation is therefore objective-specific even though the outer SimpleTES procedure is unchanged.

#### O.2 Combinatorial construction

Combinatorial construction tasks search over finite structured solutions: integer sets, unequal-circle packings, and { − 1 , 1 } \{-1,1\} sign matrices. Their evaluators are exact or feasibility-based, using integer arithmetic for sumsets, geometric non-overlap checks for packings, and exact determinant computation for sign matrices.

##### Sum–Difference Problem.

The Sum–Difference Problem comes from classical additive combinatorics and is closely related to more-sums-than-differences sets and Ruzsa-type inequalities [ 92 , 54 , 116 , 117 ] .

Candidate sets contain at most 512 integers with elements bounded in [ − 10 6 , 10 6 ] [-10^{6},10^{6}] . The evaluator computes | A + A | |A+A| and | A − A | |A-A| exactly using integer arithmetic.

Best results and discovered solutions. The discovered set has a highly regular arithmetic backbone rather than an irregular collection of integers. Most consecutive gaps follow a long progression pattern, while a small number of fringe positions receive local corrections. These sparse perturbations enlarge the sumset more efficiently than the difference set, producing a construction that is both high-scoring and structurally interpretable ( Figure 10 e).

Case Analysis. The strongest program maintains exact sum and difference multiplicity tables during search. It alternates aggressive pruning, greedy additions, and local replacements, allowing each candidate edit to be scored by its exact effect on the two set expansions. This is more efficient than repeatedly rebuilding A + A A+A and A − A A-A from scratch, and it makes the search sensitive to local fringe edits that would be difficult to identify from the final scalar score alone.

##### Circle Packing in a Unit Square.

Unequal-circle packing in a square is a classical problem in continuous optimization and discrete geometry [ 105 , 136 , 55 ] .

Invalid configurations receive zero score.

Best results and discovered solutions. The n = 26 n=26 and n = 32 n=32 solutions recover the strongest known packing values under the same evaluator, but the two instances have visibly different structure. The n = 26 n=26 construction contains a dominant central circle, large boundary anchors, and a near-triangular ring of medium circles. The n = 32 n=32 construction is more homogeneous and resembles a fixed six-row quasi-hexagonal layout. These structures show that the search does not only adjust radii locally; it recovers global geometric organization suitable for each instance size ( Figure 10 g,h).

Case Analysis. The evolved n = 26 n=26 solver uses a coarse-to-fine strategy: broad center-set exploration first identifies promising layouts, and exact radius optimization then solves the radius subproblem under boundary and pairwise-distance constraints. The n = 32 n=32 solver simplifies to a more direct routine by reusing an incumbent or fixed quasi-hexagonal layout, then solving for radii through linear programming. The two programs therefore adapt the search granularity to the instance: broader geometric exploration for n = 26 n=26 and more efficient radius refinement for n = 32 n=32 .

##### Hadamard maximum-determinant problem of order 29 29 .

The Hadamard maximum-determinant problem of order 29 29 is a classical benchmark in extremal matrix theory and D D -optimal design.

Order 29 lies outside the Hadamard regime, so the best known solutions are near-extremal sign matrices rather than exact Hadamard matrices. The evaluator computes the determinant exactly using the Bareiss algorithm and reports both the raw determinant and a normalized score.

Best results and discovered solutions. The discovered sign matrices recover the long-standing classical lower-bound record. Their structure is best inspected through the Gram matrix rather than raw signs, since row and column permutations and sign flips can change the visible matrix while preserving equivalence. The high-determinant matrices exhibit near-orthogonal Gram structure, with off-diagonal interactions controlled so that rows remain as close to mutually orthogonal as possible under the order-29 constraint ( Figure 10 f).

Case Analysis. The strongest solver expands the initial search beyond a single quadratic-residue construction. It builds a diverse seed pool containing incumbent, quadratic-residue circulant, orthogonal-sign, and cropped Sylvester-style matrices, then alternates inverse-guided multi-flip hill climbing with simulated annealing. This combination allows the program to make coordinated sign changes rather than relying on isolated single-entry flips, which is essential for escaping low-determinant basins while preserving the global Gram structure needed for a near-extremal matrix.
