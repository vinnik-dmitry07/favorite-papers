# Why Does Self-Distillation (Sometimes) Degrade the Reasoning Capability of LLMs?

###### Abstract

Self-distillation has emerged as an effective post-training paradigm for LLMs, often improving performance while shortening reasoning traces. However, in mathematical reasoning, we find that it can reduce response length while degrading performance. We trace this degradation to the suppression of epistemic verbalization —the model’s expression of uncertainty during reasoning. Through controlled experiments varying conditioning context richness and task coverage , we show that conditioning the teacher on rich information suppresses uncertainty expression, enabling rapid in-domain optimization with limited task coverage but harming OOD performance, where unseen problems benefit from expressing uncertainty and adjusting accordingly. Across Qwen3-1.7B/8B, DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-7B, and Olmo3-7B-Instruct, we observe performance drops of up to 40%. Our findings highlight that exposing appropriate levels of uncertainty is crucial for robust reasoning and underscore the importance of optimizing reasoning behavior beyond merely reinforcing correct answer traces.

## 1 Introduction

Recently, self-distillation ( Snell et al., 2022 ) has attracted increasing attention in the post-training of large language models (LLMs). In this paradigm, two instances of the same model are employed: one conditioned on privileged information (e.g., ground-truth solutions) serves as a teacher, providing dense reward signals for responses generated by another instance that does not have access to that information. Several studies have demonstrated that combining this framework with post-training methods such as Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) leads to highly efficient performance gains ( Zhu et al., 2026 ; Hübotter et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Penaloza et al., 2026 ; Liu et al., 2026 ; Ye et al., 2026 ) . These methods have shown particularly strong improvements in domains such as agentic environments and scientific reasoning, especially under in-domain evaluation settings. Interestingly, a consistent trend observed across these works is that performance improves as response length decreases, suggesting that self-distillation promotes more concise and effective reasoning.

However, when we apply the same self-distillation approach to mathematical reasoning tasks, we observe a markedly different phenomenon. Figure 1 compares the effects of a representative self-distillation algorithm, SDPO, in the Chemistry domain (a) and the Math domain (b). As shown in the figure, in the Chemistry domain, self-distillation substantially reduces response length compared to GRPO while rapidly improving performance. In contrast, in the Math domain, although response length consistently decreases as training progresses, performance drops significantly, contrary to prior findings.

This raises a question: ”Why does performance sometimes degrade despite the model being trained to move toward the correct answer?”

Our analysis reveals a consistent pattern: the more informative the context provided to the teacher, the more concise and confident the resulting reasoning becomes, with substantially fewer expressions of uncertainty and, particularly in math reasoning, degraded performance. We trace this effect to the suppression of epistemic verbalization ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , whereby models explicitly verbalize and incorporate uncertainty during reasoning. Strong reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 ( Guo et al., 2025 ) frequently express uncertainty using tokens like “Wait” or “Hmm”. Although these expressions may not directly advance the reasoning, removing them discards important signals that a reasoning path may be flawed, leading to significant performance drops ( Kim et al., 2026 ) .

To systematically understand when and why self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbalization and thus affects performance, we conduct a comprehensive empirical study and identify two key factors: information richness and task coverage . The more privileged the information the teacher receives, such as the correct solution, the more its reasoning traces suppress expressions of uncertainty, encouraging the student to imitate a confident reasoning style that presupposes information unavailable at inference time. When task coverage is limited, this compression enables rapid in-domain optimization. However, as coverage increases, removing epistemic verbalization can interfere with optimization across diverse tasks, degrading performance on more challenging or unseen problems.

More broadly, our results show that even when the training objective faithfully guides the model toward correct reasoning traces, the resulting reasoning style can quietly shift in ways that hurt generalization. The suppression of epistemic verbalization is not penalized by standard objectives, yet negatively impacts out-of-distribution (OOD) performance. This suggests that post-training objectives need to account not only for answer correctness, but also for eliciting and preserving uncertainty-aware reasoning behaviors. We believe these findings offer a useful step toward a deeper understanding of reasoning in self-distillation and post-training more broadly.

## 2 Preliminaries

#### Self-Distillation

Let x ∈ 𝒳 x\in\mathcal{X} denote an input and y = ( y 1 , … , y T ) y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T}) a sequence generated by a language model π θ \pi_{\theta} . #### Key Characteristics of Math Reasoning

In LLMs, math reasoning can be viewed as self-Bayesian reasoning , where each step is generated conditioned only on the prompt x x and previously generated tokens y = ( y 1 , … , y T ) y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{T}) , allowing the model to iteratively update its belief over intermediate hypotheses ( Kim et al., 2026 ) . Such reasoning spans diverse tasks such as arithmetic, algebra, geometry, word problems, and logical pattern recognition, so evaluation benchmarks are frequently OOD relative to training data due to compositional and reasoning-depth shifts.

Within this process, verbalized uncertainty toward correct response y ∗ y^{*} , referred to as epistemic verbalization ( Kim et al., 2026 ) , can serve as an informative signal rather than stylistic redundancy. As shown in Figure 2(2a) , reasoning without such signals may prematurely commit to incorrect hypotheses with little room for recovery, whereas epistemic verbalization preserves alternative hypotheses and supports gradual uncertainty reduction.

## 3 LLM Reasoning Behavior Under Richer Information

In self-distillation, the teacher has access to a richer context c c , which provides strong hints and enables it to generate reasoning traces with minimal expressed uncertainty (Figure 2(2b) ). While this yields more concise responses, it could eliminate not only unnecessary verbosity but also valuable epistemic signals, potentially hindering the student’s ability to perform uncertainty-aware reasoning. This raises two questions we investigate: (i) how does the richness of c c relate to the suppression of uncertainty expression and to reasoning performance? and (ii) if epistemic verbalization is beneficial, why does suppressing it improve performance in domains such as Chemistry while degrading it in Math?

To study these questions, we first need a way to quantify how informative the conditioning context is. Let y ∗ y^{*} denote a random variable representing an ideal correct response to x x . We formalize the information that c c provides about y ∗ y^{*} as the conditional mutual information I ⁡ ( y ∗ ; c ∣ x ) = H ⁡ ( y ∗ ∣ x ) − H ⁡ ( y ∗ ∣ x , c ) , I(y^{*};\,c\mid x)\;=\;H(y^{*}\mid x)\;-\;H(y^{*}\mid x,\,c), (2) which captures the reduction in uncertainty about y ∗ y^{*} once the additional context c c is given.

Building on this measure, we construct a controlled comparison to examine how the model’s reasoning behavior changes as the conditioning information is varied. We use the DAPO-Math-17k dataset ( Yu et al., 2026 ) with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B ( Guo et al., 2025 ) as the base model, selecting 100 problems on which the base model achieves accuracy between 0.125 and 0.5 over 8 rollouts. ## 4 Supervised Finetuning with Self-Distillation

A natural follow-up question is whether the suppression of epistemic verbalization under high I ⁡ ( y ∗ ; c ∣ x ) I(y^{*};\,c\mid x) is merely stylistic or has a tangible impact on reasoning capability. To test this, we conduct off-policy self-distillation (SFT) using DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B ( Guo et al., 2025 ) on two datasets, each containing 800 correct responses:

• 𝒟 ug \mathcal{D}_{\text{ug}} : unguided responses ( c = ∅ c=\emptyset ), with high 𝔼 ⁡ [ E ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[E(y)] and 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] ≈ 12 ​ k \mathbb{E}[L(y)]\approx 12\text{k} tokens.

• 𝒟 sg \mathcal{D}_{\text{sg}} : solution-guided responses ( c = s c=s ), with low 𝔼 ⁡ [ E ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[E(y)] and 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] ≈ 2 ​ k \mathbb{E}[L(y)]\approx 2\text{k} tokens.

Both datasets consist entirely of correct trajectories. The key difference lies in the epistemic density of the training signal. We evaluate the resulting checkpoints across multiple math benchmarks (examples from each dataset are presented in Appendix K.1 ).

As shown in Table 2 , training on 𝒟 sg \mathcal{D}_{\text{sg}} leads to substantial degradation across all benchmarks, despite the dataset consisting of correct answers, whereas training on 𝒟 ug \mathcal{D}_{\text{ug}} produces no significant performance change. This asymmetry arises because solution-guided responses are concise precisely due to the external context s s . Using them as SFT targets forces the model to imitate a reasoning style that presupposes information unavailable at inference time, effectively suppressing the epistemic tokens that support autonomous exploration and error correction.

## 5 On-Policy Self-Distillation

We now turn to on-policy self-distillation ( Hübotter et al., 2026 ; Zhao et al., 2026 ; Penaloza et al., 2026 ) , where the model learns from dense reward signals provided by a self-teacher with access to the correct solution, based on its own responses. We compare GRPO with Reinforcement Learning via Self-Distillation (SDPO) ( Hübotter et al., 2026 ) on the DAPO-Math-17k dataset ( Yu et al., 2026 ) , using Qwen3-8B ( Yang et al., 2025 ) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B ( Guo et al., 2025 ) . Additional results for Qwen3-1.7B and Olmo-3-7B-Instruct ( Olmo et al., 2025 ) are in Appendix J.3 . For each model, we track training score, response length, and OOD performance on AIME24 and AMC23. We fix the teacher to the initial policy, which performs better than a moving target (see Section 5.5 for a comparison).

The behavior of on-policy self-distillation depends on two factors: (i) the extent of epistemic verbalization already present in the base model, and (ii) the richness of the conditioning context c c . To disentangle these, we compare GRPO and SDPO under two settings: c = s c=s (full solution) and c = s ∖ think c=s_{\setminus\text{think}} (solution without <think> content).

### 5.1 DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B is a representative high-reasoning model that generates rich epistemic verbalizations within <think> tags, averaging about 180 epistemic tokens per response, as shown in Section 3 , leading to lengthy yet high-performing reasoning outputs.

#### Training Performance

As shown in Figure 3a , GRPO training slightly increases 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] with a modest improvement in score. In contrast, SDPO with c = s c=s causes a sharp initial drop in both 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] and score. Performance gradually recovers but remains below GRPO throughout training. When the conditioning is reduced to c = s ∖ think c=s_{\setminus\text{think}} , the drop in 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] is attenuated and the score trajectory approaches that of GRPO, consistent with the relationship between I ⁡ ( y ∗ ; c ∣ x ) I(y^{*};\,c\mid x) and epistemic suppression discussed in Section 3 .

Reducing the conditioning to c = s ∖ think c=s_{\setminus\text{think}} mitigates the drop, though performance still remains below the base model.

### 5.2 Qwen3-8B (Thinking Mode: ON)

Qwen3-8B is a hybrid reasoning model that can operate in either a thinking mode for deliberate reasoning or a non-thinking mode for direct responses. With thinking mode enabled, Qwen3-8B produces longer responses than DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, with a higher number of epistemic tokens (see Appendix E.2 for a comparison of epistemic tokens across models).

#### Training Performance

As shown in Figure 4a , 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] decreases under both GRPO and SDPO, with SDPO exhibiting a larger reduction and a correspondingly larger performance drop. Notably, 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] first drops sharply, then increases slightly. As the context becomes less informative, the model compensates by increasing epistemic verbalization, causing the length to partially recover.

#### OOD Evaluation - AIME24, AMC23

The gap becomes more pronounced on OOD benchmarks (Figures 4b and 4c ): GRPO maintains largely stable performance with gradually decreasing 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] , whereas SDPO falls below the base model, particularly with c = s c=s . Figure 4d reveals that both methods reduce 𝔼 ⁡ [ E ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[E(y)] relative to the base model, with SDPO more aggressively so. This suggests that Qwen3-8B originally generates more epistemic verbalization than necessary. While both methods mitigate this redundancy, overly aggressive suppression risks removing epistemic signals that carry useful reasoning information.

### 5.3 Qwen3-8B (Thinking Mode: OFF)

When Qwen3-8B is used without thinking mode, the <think> tag is absent, so we consider only c = s c=s . The model initially produces much shorter responses and exhibits significantly lower performance. In contrast, SDPO reduces 𝔼 ⁡ [ L ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[L(y)] and improves much more slowly. ### 5.4 Causal Study: Epistemic Token Suppression during GRPO

To further examine whether the suppression of epistemic verbalization is a causal contributor to the observed performance degradation, we explicitly penalized epistemic tokens during GRPO training and tested whether this intervention induces a similar degradation pattern.

Following the epistemic marker set 𝒯 \mathcal{T} defined in Section 3 , we override the standard GRPO advantage at every position t t whose generated token realizes a marker in 𝒯 \mathcal{T} , setting A ~ t = min ⁡ ( A t , − κ ) \tilde{A}_{t}=\min(A_{t},-\kappa) if y t ∈ 𝒯 y_{t}\in\mathcal{T} and A ~ t = A t \tilde{A}_{t}=A_{t} otherwise, with suppression strength κ = 0.5 \kappa=0.5 . Since A ~ t ≤ − κ \tilde{A}_{t}\leq-\kappa holds even for correct rollouts, the policy gradient consistently reduces 𝔼 ⁡ [ E ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[E(y)] throughout training.

As shown in Figure 6 , explicitly penalizing epistemic tokens during GRPO produces shorter responses and lower performance, qualitatively reproducing the degradation pattern observed with SDPO. The degradation is less pronounced than under SDPO, possibly because the predefined epistemic token set does not capture all linguistic realizations of uncertainty or epistemic reasoning, as discussed further in Appendix E.5 . Nevertheless, this result provides causal evidence that suppressing uncertainty expression contributes to the performance decline under SDPO.

### 5.5 Ablation Study: Fixed vs. Moving Target Teacher

In naive on-policy self-distillation, the teacher and student share a continuously updated policy, making the teacher a moving target that can introduce training instability ( Zhao et al., 2026 ; Ye et al., 2026 ) . To mitigate this, SDPO uses an EMA-smoothed teacher (EMA rate: 0.05). However, we find that setting the EMA rate to 0.0 (i.e., fixing the teacher to the initial policy) yields better performance, and thus Section 5 follows this setting.

Figure 7a shows additional comparison results when the teacher is updated during training. As shown, even slow updates (e.g., rate 0.05) lead to a sharper reduction in response length, resulting in larger performance degradation. This can be interpreted as a feedback loop in self-distillation. The model is trained to produce increasingly confident outputs, and when this is used as the teacher, it generates even more confident responses, amplifying the effect over iterations. Further ablations on learning rate, top- k k logits, and the effect of limiting the maximum response length to 4,096 tokens are provided in Appendix D .

## 6 Relationship Between Task Coverage, Epistemic Verbalization and Generalization Ability

Across the off-policy and on-policy settings analyzed above, self-distillation consistently produces more confident responses with reduced 𝔼 ⁡ [ E ⁡ ( y ) ] \mathbb{E}[E(y)] . This aligns with the findings of Hübotter et al. (2026) , which reports that SDPO learns to reason concisely: on Science Q&A (Chemistry, Physics, Biology, and Materials Science) ( Feng et al., 2024 ) , tool use ( Tang et al., 2023 ) , and LiveCodeBench v6 ( Jain et al., 2025 ) , SDPO achieves higher accuracy than GRPO while producing substantially shorter outputs with fewer epistemic markers.

In other words, in these domains, self-distillation suppresses epistemic verbalization and improves performance simultaneously. The key question is why the same mechanism leads to performance degradation in our math-focused setup . We hypothesize that the answer lies in differences in task coverage between the training and evaluation distributions.

### 6.1 Comparison of Task Coverage

To test this hypothesis, we compare the dataset characteristics of the settings where SDPO outperformed GRPO against our experimental setup. As shown in Table 3 , the Chemistry dataset, despite its large size, draws from only six main problem types that differ primarily in surface details rather than underlying structure. LiveCodeBench v6 contains diverse problems but only 131 in total, leading to repeated exposure during training with identical train/eval splits. In contrast, DAPO-Math-17k exposes the model to 14,000 distinct problems (78% of the 25,600 samples drawn over 100 steps, due to repeated sampling), spanning a broad, non-overlapping range of problem types, and evaluation is performed on unseen problem types.

### 6.2 Relationship Between Task Coverage and Learning Performance

To further investigate the interplay between task coverage and generalization, we vary the number of training questions | 𝒟 | ∈ { 1 , 8 , 64 , 128 , 512 } |\mathcal{D}|\in\{1,8,64,128,512\} from DAPO-Math-17k and train with both GRPO and SDPO. All experiments use Qwen3-8B (Thinking Mode OFF).

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we provide an empirical analysis of on-policy self-distillation, motivated by an information-theoretic view of conditioning context richness. Our experiments suggest that the effectiveness of self-distillation is closely tied to how information is provided to the model and how the model expresses uncertainty during reasoning. We observe that self-distillation tends to produce answers with higher confidence and shorter reasoning traces. While this effect enables more compact reasoning and can quickly improve in-domain performance when task coverage is limited, it becomes less effective when task coverage is broad and may even harm OOD performance.

Importantly, the fact that performance can degrade even when a mathematically sound objective function is designed to elicit correct CoT reasoning suggests that the choice of optimization objective alone may not be sufficient for preserving robust reasoning, and that we need to pay closer attention to how training reshapes the model’s reasoning behavior, beyond answer correctness. We hope that our analysis contributes to a deeper understanding of self-distillation and other LLM post-training methods, and provides insights for developing more robust training strategies.

## Ethics Statement

This work aims to analyze the causes of reasoning performance degradation during self-distillation of LLMs. All experiments were conducted using publicly available models (Qwen3, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen, OLMo) and datasets (DAPO-Math-17k, AIME, AMC, MATH500). As this study does not involve any new data collection or human subjects research, concerns related to privacy or human subjects ethics do not apply.

A key finding of this work is that self-distillation can suppress epistemic verbalization, thereby diminishing a model’s ability to express uncertainty during reasoning. This observation carries important implications for the design of LLM post-training methods. The ability to appropriately express uncertainty is directly related to model reliability and safety. Training procedures that indiscriminately suppress such expressions may increase the risk of models producing incorrect answers with unwarranted confidence. We hope that our analysis contributes to the development of more robust and trustworthy reasoning models.

## Appendix A Related Works

#### Self-Distillation for LLM Reasoning.

Self-distillation, where a single model acts as both teacher and student under asymmetric conditioning ( Snell et al., 2022 ) , has recently been widely adopted for LLM post-training. SDPO ( Hübotter et al., 2026 ) conditions the self-teacher on its previously generated correct trajectories or rich environment feedback to provide dense credit assignment, while OPSD ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) leverages ground-truth solutions as privileged information, achieving strong efficiency gains in both cases. Our analysis builds upon the SDPO codebase, and a detailed comparison with OPSD is provided in Appendix I . Liu et al. (2026) ; Penaloza et al. (2026) ; Ye et al. (2026) further utilize this paradigm in settings that incorporate additional signals, such as privileged information, self-generated hints derived from environmental interactions, or contextual guidance, with the goal of internalizing this external knowledge into the model’s parameters.

While these works demonstrate the effectiveness of self-distillation, they do not investigate when or why self-distillation may degrade reasoning performance, especially in scenarios where a model must solve problems entirely on its own, without interaction with the external environment, which is the central question of our work.

#### Uncertainty Expression in LLM Reasoning.

Kim et al. (2026) introduce an information-theoretic framework that identifies epistemic verbalization, the explicit externalization of uncertainty, as informationally necessary for robust reasoning. Our work answers a question this framework cannot readily explain: Under what conditions is this behavior encouraged or suppressed? And if epistemic verbalization is necessary, why does suppressing it lead to better performance in some domains, such as Chemistry, while degrading performance in others? We trace this to two factors: conditioning context richness (Section 3 ) and task coverage (Section 6 ), supported by a systematic analysis of recent on-policy self-distillation methods across various models with varying pretrained capabilities.

#### Reasoning Compression.

Several recent methods aim to shorten reasoning traces while preserving accuracy. GFPO ( Shrivastava et al., 2026 ) filters training rollouts by length and token efficiency within enlarged sample groups, reducing GRPO’s length inflation by 46–85% on STEM and coding benchmarks. OPSDC ( Sang et al., 2026 ) uses a conciseness instruction as privileged context, achieving 57–59% token reduction on MATH-500. ConPress ( Deng et al., 2026 ) exploits multi-question contextual pressure to elicit self-compressed traces, yielding 30–60% reductions via SFT. Accordion-Thinking ( Yang et al., 2026 ) trains models to produce periodic step summaries that can be discarded, achieving 3 × \times throughput with a gap-vanishing phenomenon during RL training. CEEH ( Luo et al., 2026 ) proposes difficulty-aware entropy regularization to prevent entropy collapse under length constraints.

Our analysis complements these efforts by showing that compression which suppresses uncertainty expression, even when producing correct answers, can harm OOD generalization, and that this effect is modulated by task coverage. Therefore, the key challenge is to filter out non-informative content while retaining epistemic expressions that enable iterative belief refinement, rather than blindly compressing the reasoning process.

## Appendix B Basis for Selecting Epistemic Tokens

To provide an empirical basis for our selection of epistemic tokens, we analyzed uncertainty expressions across a diverse set of reasoning models. We collected 9,600 reasoning traces from eight models spanning four model families, including the Qwen3 family ( Yang et al., 2025 ) (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-32B), the QwQ family ( Yang et al., 2025 ) (QwQ-32B), the DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen family ( Guo et al., 2025 ) (7B and 32B), and the OLMo family ( Olmo et al., 2025 ) (OLMo-3-7B-Instruct).

The traces were generated on 600 math problems drawn from AIME 2024/2025, AMC 2023, and MATH500, with two samples per problem for each model. Each reasoning trace was then provided to GPT-5 with the following prompt:

This procedure yielded over 99,000 ( span , trigger_token ) (\textit{span},\textit{trigger\_token}) pairs. We then ranked trigger tokens by frequency within each model family (Qwen3, QwQ, DeepSeek, and OLMo).

As shown in Table 4 , the ten markers maybe , wait , check , hmm , seems , perhaps , might , actually , likely , and alternatively , together with their natural variants, consistently appeared among the top-ranked uncertainty expressions, supporting their inclusion across model families. While several tokens, such as let me try/verify , I think , and make sure , were excluded, our selected markers cover 75–93% of the occurrences of the phrases in Table 4 .

Moreover, to account for uncertainty expressions not captured by our token set, we conducted an independent LLM-as-a-Judge annotation experiment (Appendix E.5 ) that captures the full range of uncertainty expressions. The resulting distribution closely mirrors that obtained with our token-based approach.

Finally, although the linguistic patterns used to express uncertainty vary slightly across models, Qwen3, QwQ, and DeepSeek exhibit highly similar distributions. OLMo also shares largely the same set of uncertainty tokens, but differs in the relative frequency with which individual tokens are used. The selected markers largely overlap with expressions that human readers would naturally recognize as signals of uncertainty, plausibly because these models are ultimately trained on human-generated text.

## Appendix G Analysis of Changes in Model Internal Uncertainty under Self-Distillation

To examine whether self-distillation also reduces the model’s internal uncertainty, we further analyze token-level entropy as a measure of internal uncertainty. Building on the analysis in Section 3 , we revisit the comparison between unguided and solution-guided generation to assess whether access to richer information is associated with lower predictive uncertainty.

As shown in Table 13 , solution-guided generation reduces average token-level entropy by approximately 43%, indicating substantially lower predictive uncertainty when richer information is available. Moreover, under unguided generation, epistemic tokens have an average entropy of 1.34, approximately 3.5 × 3.5\times higher than the overall average, indicating that epistemic verbalization tends to occur at positions of high predictive uncertainty.

We next examine whether this lower-entropy generation behavior is transferred to the student through self-distillation.

Table 14 and Figure 17 show a consistent pattern in which GRPO increases token-level entropy for both models, whereas SDPO reduces it, and this distinction persists throughout training. These results indicate that the suppression of uncertainty expressions through self-distillation is accompanied by a genuine reduction in the model’s internal uncertainty.

## Appendix H Can Uncertainty-Encouraging Prompts Mitigate the Suppression?

We further investigate whether prompting the teacher to produce more verbose reasoning with explicit expressions of uncertainty can mitigate the performance degradation caused by the suppression of uncertainty expressions. To this end, we design a modified regeneration prompt that encourages detailed, step-by-step reasoning with uncertainty, and repeat the reasoning behavior analysis (Section 3 ) and on-policy self-distillation experiments (Section 5 ) using this prompt.

Table 15 shows that the uncertainty-encouraging prompt partially recovers the suppressed reasoning behavior: compared to the vanilla solution-guided prompt, it produces longer responses (3,098 vs. 1,873 tokens) and more epistemic expressions (31.4 vs. 8.8). However, both metrics remain far below those of unguided generation, indicating that the presence of a reference solution fundamentally constrains the model’s reasoning verbosity regardless of the prompt instruction.

Figure 18 shows that the uncertainty-encouraging prompt leads to slightly higher training scores and longer responses compared to the vanilla setting, and this improvement carries over to AIME24 evaluation. Nevertheless, both variants still suffer from substantial performance degradation, suggesting that prompting alone is insufficient to resolve the fundamental issue of uncertainty suppression in solution-guided generation.

## Appendix I Comparison with OPSD from Zhao et al. (2026)

Recently, Zhao et al. (2026) proposed OPSD and demonstrated performance gains in mathematical reasoning through on-policy self-distillation, particularly on the Qwen3 series. Unlike our setup, where both the student and the teacher either enable or disable thinking mode, OPSD adopts a hybrid configuration in which the student operates with thinking mode disabled while the teacher has it enabled.

As our experiments also confirm, enabling thinking mode produces substantially longer responses with a greater number of epistemic tokens, making this hybrid setup function more akin to conventional teacher–student distillation, despite using the same underlying model. We note that this configuration is inherently limited to model families such as Qwen3 that support toggling thinking mode on and off.

For training efficiency, OPSD trains only on a response prefix (1,024 tokens by default), rather than the full student response. Unlike OPSD, SDPO and our setup use full fine-tuning with verl ( Sheng et al., 2024 ) , whereas OPSD adopts LoRA fine-tuning with trl ( von Werra et al., 2020 ) and a smaller batch size of 32 (vs. 256 in our setup), resulting in higher training efficiency but smaller parameter updates per step.

Under this hybrid setup with prefix learning in Qwen3-1.7B, as shown in Figure 19a ( orange line ), we observe an intriguing training dynamic. In the early stages, the thinking-enabled teacher encourages the student to generate longer responses while simultaneously improving performance, highlighting the effectiveness of hybrid distillation during the initial phase of training. However, as training progresses, performance steadily deteriorates despite this early improvement. In contrast, under our homogeneous setup, where both the student and teacher operate with thinking mode enabled ( pink line ), performance declines consistently throughout training. Response length and epistemic token count likewise decrease steadily, consistent with our analysis.

While this hybrid distillation setup for the Qwen3 series presents an interesting research direction with its own unique training dynamics, such as why performance initially improves before declining and whether this stems from changes in reasoning behavior or chat template mismatch, a thorough investigation is beyond the scope of this work and is left for future exploration.

Find the number of ways to place a digit in each cell of a 2 × \times 3 grid so that the sum of the two numbers formed by reading left to right is 999, and the sum of the three numbers formed by reading top to bottom is 99.
