# To Retain or to Adapt? Generalizing Continual Learning

###### Abstract

The Continual Learning (CL) literature has long been driven by the goal of mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This objective rests on a pervasive, often unstated assumption: that a lifelong learner should approximate the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution and retain all previously acquired knowledge. We challenge this retention-centered premise, arguing that in non-stationary environments prioritizing retention can impede real-time adaptation. Shifting the focus to the Average Lifelong Error (ALE), we formalize CL as an online optimization problem governed by the interaction between environmental and learning dynamics. We introduce Transfer Efficiency as a quantitative measure of the tension between Instability , the bias inherited from conflicting past experience, and Transient Error , the optimization cost of learning new tasks from scratch. Under mild convergence conditions, holding across linear and neural network models, this decomposition yields a Critical Task Duration : a closed-form threshold beyond which historical knowledge transitions from a warm-start advantage to an optimization liability whenever retention induces a positive stationary bias. We validate these theoretical predictions on continual image classification and reinforcement learning benchmarks. Finally, by connecting continual learning to the online learning framework of predictable sequences, we show that JTL is only one instance of a broader family of objectives, and we propose a new general class of continual learning algorithms, which we call Predictive Continual Learning . Predictive CL algorithms optimize expected future performance under an explicit, dynamically updated model of future tasks. As a proof of concept, we analyze a Window algorithm that interpolates between JTL and Independent-Task Learning (ITL), outperforming both under controlled distributional drift.

### 1 Introduction

Real-world environments are inherently nonstationary, evolving over time and requiring systems that can adapt continually and do so efficiently. Continual learning (CL) ( Ring, 1994 ; Thrun and Mitchell, 1995 ; Parisi et al., 2019 ; Hadsell et al., 2020 , e.g.) —also known as lifelong learning—addresses this challenge by developing algorithms that can acquire, retain, and update knowledge over extended sequences of experience. Unlike conventional machine learning, where the data distribution is fixed, continual learning operates under distributional drift, where performance must be maintained relative to a moving target.

Progress in continual learning has historically been guided and measured by a diverse list of metrics, which reflect the many desiderata of an adaptive agent, namely: forward transfer , minimal forgetting , preservation of plasticity , and computational efficiency ( Schwarz et al., 2018 ; Hadsell et al., 2020 ; Mundt et al., 2023 ) . This distinction of separate metrics has inevitably led to the development of methods focused only on one or a subset of those, at the expense of the other desiderata ( Wołczyk et al., 2021 ; Dohare et al., 2024 ) . Recently, Kumar et al. (2025) formally proved that maximizing real-time performance naturally leads to a trade-off between these requirements—one that is driven dynamically by the environment rather than fixed a priori. Their proposed Average Lifelong Error (ALE) objective expresses continual learning as the minimization of the online expected error in a dynamic environment, subject to resource constraints. Importantly, this perspective frames forgetting as a functional necessity to adapt within finite computations. As Kumar et al. (2025) shows, a learner with limited compute must selectively retain only the information predictive of the future , and systematically discard the rest to maintain adaptive capacity.

In the real world, it is difficult to know in advance which piece of past information will be useful in the future. Consequently, any lifelong learning algorithm must rely on assumptions or an explicit model about the future. We argue that algorithms designed to preserve performance on all previously encountered tasks, including most continual learning methods aimed at minimizing catastrophic forgetting, implicitly encode such a model. Specifically, the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) objective—the average loss over all tasks observed up to the current time— corresponds to a particular assumption about future structure: the future tasks are drawn from the same distribution as past tasks. Equivalently, JTL is the special case of ALE objective under an ergodic environment, where there statistical properties of the task stream remain stationary over time.

While this assumption can be reasonable in many scenarios, e.g. when the agent gradually explores a larger but fundamentally stable world, it breaks down when the environment changes irreversibly and past data become progressively less predictive. In such settings, optimizing the JTL objective can place excessive weight on outdated information, hindering adaptation and yielding suboptimal lifelong performance.

To make this perspective concrete, we contrast the JTL formulation with the Independent-Task Learning (ITL) objective, which optimizes only on the current task and retain no information from past tasks. This comparison highlights that neither objective is universally optimal. In rapidly changing environments, the ITL objective can achieve lower lifelong error by avoiding the implicit bias toward past distributions. Conversely, when past experience remains predictive of the future, the JTL objective benefits from this bias to accelerate learning and improve generalization. By analyzing these two extremes in both controlled quadratic models and deep neural networks, we characterize the conditions under which memory becomes a liability for learning. This analysis introduces three key concepts: Transfer Efficiency , which quantifies this trade-off; Instability , which measures the cost of bias inherited from previous tasks; and Transient Error , which captures the cost of learning a new task from scratch.

Our framework connects several adjacent literatures—online learning, multi-task learning, and learning to learn— revealing a shared theoretical foundation. It offers analytical tools to challenge and extend existing theories. In particular, the connection to the online learning literature allows us to interpret the JTL and ITL objectives as part of a family of algorithms based on the concept of predictable sequences ( Rakhlin and Sridharan, 2013 ) . In light of this connection, we propose a generalization of continual learning algorithms beyond the restrictive assumption of ergodicity. Rather than optimizing for perfect retention or complete adaptation, the objective becomes minimizing the expected future error under a model of future tasks, where the predictive model itself may be learned and updated online. We refer to algorithms that optimize this generalized objective as Predictive Continual Learning (Predictive CL) . This formulation transforms the space between JTL and ITL from a gap between two extremes into a principled design space, enabling continual learning algorithms to adapt their retention strategy to the structure of the environment rather than to a fixed, a priori objective.

###### Contributions.

We summarize the main contributions of this paper as follows: 1. We recast continual learning as an online optimization problem centered on Average Lifelong Error (ALE), showing that the widely adopted Joint-Task Learning (JTL) objective is not a universally optimal target in non-stationary environments, but rather corresponds to a particular assumption about future task structure.

2. We formalize CL as the interaction between environment dynamics and learning dynamics, revealing that the utility of memory depends jointly on task distributions, optimization dynamics, and model properties.

3. We introduce three analytical quantities: Transfer Efficiency , Instability , and Transient Error that decompose the benefit of transferring past knowledge. This decomposition yields a Critical Task Duration under which retained knowledge transitions from accelerating learning to hindering adaptation.

4. We derive closed-form expressions for these quantities in quadratic objectives, showing that the environment Growth Rate determines the asymptotic benefits of long term memory. We further demonstrate that the same mechanisms emerge empirically in deep linear models, continual image classification, and reinforcement learning benchmarks.

5. We connect continual learning to the online learning literature, unifying JTL and ITL within a broader family of objectives. Building on this perspective, we propose Predictive CL , a generalized framework in which algorithms optimize expected future performance under an explicit predictive model of future tasks. As a proof of concept, we analyze a Window algorithm that interpolates between ITL and JTL and outperforms both under controlled distributional drift.

### 2 Background & Setup

#### 2.1 A General Objective for Continual Learning

We begin by recalling the continual learning objective introduced by Kumar et al. (2025) , and instantiate it in the supervised learning setting. 1 1 1 We adopt a slightly different notation from the original formulation to better align with the continual learning literature. We review the main concepts here and provide an in-depth exposition in the supplementary material ( Section A.1 ).

###### General setting.

During its lifetime, an agent accumulates a history H T = { ( a 0 , o 1 ) , … , ( a T − 1 , o T ) } H_{T}=\{(a_{0},o_{1}),\dots,(a_{T-1},o_{T})\} of action-observation pairs in A × O A\times O . At each time step n n the agent samples an action a n ∼ π n ( ⋅ ∣ H n ) a_{n}\sim\pi_{n}(\cdot\mid H_{n}) from a policy π n \pi_{n} conditioned on the history. The environment then produces a new observation o n + 1 ∼ 𝒫 ( ⋅ ∣ H n , a n ) o_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot\mid H_{n},a_{n}) , and the learner uses the new experience ( a n , o n + 1 ) (a_{n},o_{n+1}) together with the pre-existing history H n H_{n} to produce a new policy estimate π n + 1 \pi_{n+1} .

In this work we model the learning step as a stochastic process guided by an algorithm 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , which updates the policy according to π n ∼ 𝒜 ( ⋅ | H n ) \pi_{n}\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot|H_{n}) . Therefore, at each time step there are two interacting stochastic processes (depicted in Figure 2 ): the learning process , by which π n \pi_{n} and then a n a_{n} are sampled, and the environment process , by which o n + 1 o_{n+1} is sampled. Together, these coupled processes determine the agent’s lifetime trajectory.

The supervised learning problem is a special case of this general formulation in which the environment process is independent of the learning process —though the converse does not hold, since the learner’s policy still depends on past observations. Concretely, the new observation o n + 1 o_{n+1} is independent of the action a n a_{n} , so o n + 1 ∼ 𝒫 ( ⋅ ∣ H n ) o_{n+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot\mid H_{n}) .

Two features of this formulation are central to what follows. First, the learner is evaluated on the next-step observation o n + 1 o_{n+1} : in stationary settings this corresponds to the conventional notion of test-error, but under non-stationarity the data distribution may change in successive time steps ( 𝒫 n ≠ 𝒫 n + 1 \mathcal{P}_{n}\neq\mathcal{P}_{n+1} ), causing previously acquired knowledge to lose predictive value. Second, the computational constraint on 𝒜 \mathcal{A} induces the essential trade-off at the heart of CL. With unlimited computational resources, an agent could retain all past information while remaining adaptive. Under realistic resource constraints, it may be better to forget past data once they cease to predict the future. As we show in the following sections, these trade-offs emerge even in simple quadratic learning problems.

#### 2.2 Analysis Setup and Formalism

The general formulation above accommodates arbitrary forms of non-stationarity. Following the CL literature, we introduce a task-based abstraction, where the data-generating process (described by 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} ) is locally stationary .

###### Definition 1 (Task-based data generating process.) .

A given history H T H_{T} is a task-based sequence if the sampling distribution 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} is locally stationary. The timeline is partitioned into time intervals called tasks S k = [ a k , b k ] S_{k}=[a_{k},b_{k}] such that at each time step n ∈ S k n\in S_{k} an observation o n o_{n} is sampled from a task-specific distribution 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}^{k} .

Unless otherwise stated, we assume the history is a task-based sequence with known task switch points . We denote by z ⁡ ( n ) z(n) the task identity at time n n and by N k = | S k | N_{k}=|S_{k}| the duration of task k k .

With a slight abuse of notation we use ℓ ⁡ ( θ , o ) \ell(\theta,o) to indicate ℓ ⁡ ( π θ , o ) \ell(\pi_{\theta},o) , where θ \theta are the parameters of the policy π \pi . We denote the expected loss for task k k over the model parameters θ \theta as ℒ k ​ ( θ ) = 𝔼 o ∼ 𝒫 k ​ [ ℓ ⁡ ( θ , o ) ] \mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\mathcal{P}^{k}}[\ell(\theta,o)] We refer to the corresponding oracle minimizer θ ⋆ k ∈ arg ⁡ min θ ​ ℒ k ​ ( θ ) \theta^{k}_{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta) as a task solution vector . Defining c k = ℒ k ​ ( θ ⋆ k ) c^{k}=\mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta^{k}_{\star}) the optimal value of the loss for task k k for the chosen model class, we get the average regret of the optimization algorithm: ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ) − c k \mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A})-c^{k} . #### 2.3 The algorithm as a Markov Process

We treat the algorithm as a stochastic process. This is the most generic framing of an iterative learner: the rest of the theory in this paper instantiates it on SGD, but the definitions below cover any first-order stochastic-approximation method. More formally, we define 𝒜 \mathcal{A} to be a discrete-time stochastic process { θ n } n ≥ 0 \{\theta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} taking values in the parameter space, and we denote by Q n Q_{n} the resulting parameter distribution at time n n . The evolution of the parameters is governed by a stochastic update rule U U . In this work we consider algorithms where, at step n n , the update rule depends on the current parameters θ n \theta_{n} and a data batch ξ n \xi_{n} drawn from an algorithm-specific sampling distribution 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} : θ n + 1 = U ⁡ ( θ n , ξ n ) , where ​ ξ n ∼ 𝒟 n . \theta_{n+1}=U(\theta_{n},\xi_{n}),\quad\text{where }\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}. This formula covers SGD as our running example and, by augmenting θ n \theta_{n} to include auxiliary variables, momentum-based methods, Adam, and more broadly the entire family of stochastic approximation algorithms based on first-order Markov processes .

Crucially, the sampling distribution 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} is distinct from the environmental distribution 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} . While 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} dictates the incoming data stream, 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} dictates the data used for optimization; for replay-based algorithms—such as the JTL algorithm presented here— 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} may include data from previous tasks. Moreover, we assume the sampling strategy is consistent within task boundaries, such that 𝒟 n = 𝒟 k \mathcal{D}_{n}=\mathcal{D}^{k} for all n ∈ S k n\in S_{k} .

Importantly, the computational cost of such algorithms depends on the complexity of the update function U U . For example, for SGD the complexity is proportional to the batch size used at each step, since that determines the number of forward and backward passes through the model.

Throughout this paper we reason about Q n Q_{n} (the parameter distribution at step n n ) and its stationary limit Q ∞ Q_{\infty} ; in practice, expectations under Q n Q_{n} are estimated from the empirical ensemble of training trajectories induced by multiple random seeds, and the deep-network experiments treat each trained model as a sample from Q n Q_{n} at the corresponding step.

#### 2.4 JTL and ITL processes

Within these boundaries, many different learning algorithms can be implemented through choices of U U or 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} . Here we introduce two specific examples, that will be the paper’s object of study.

###### The Joint-Task Learning algorithm ( 𝒜 JTL \mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}} ).

The JTL agent aims to optimize the average loss over all seen tasks. This is equivalent to experience replay with an infinitely large buffer—often used in continual learning as an upper bound on performance. To maintain time-homogeneous dynamics within a task, we model the experience replay as a fixed mixture approximation. Specifically, the sampling distribution within task k k , 𝒟 J ​ T ​ L k \mathcal{D}^{k}_{JTL} , is a weighted mixture of the current and all preceding task distributions: 𝒟 J ​ T ​ L k = ∑ j = 1 k w j k ⋅ 𝒫 j , where ​ w j k = N j b k . \mathcal{D}^{k}_{JTL}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}^{k}\cdot\mathcal{P}^{j},\qquad\text{where }w_{j}^{k}=\frac{N_{j}}{b_{k}}\,.

###### The Independent-Task Learning algorithm ( 𝒜 ITL \mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}} ).

The ITL agent optimizes solely based on the current data stream. Consequently, the sampling distribution is identical to the current task distribution, 𝒟 I ​ T ​ L k = 𝒫 k . \mathcal{D}^{k}_{ITL}=\mathcal{P}^{k}\,. At the boundary between tasks, the ITL agent resets the parameters to a fixed initialization distribution, effectively discarding prior knowledge to avoid negative transfer and loss of plasticity ( Dohare et al., 2024 ) . A related variant warm-starts each task from the previous parameters instead of resetting; the differences are minor for our analysis, and we discuss them in Section A.2.2 .

###### Equivalent computational costs.

By construction, ITL and JTL share the same optimization compute per iteration: the cost scales linearly with the examples processed in a gradient update, compute ​ ( 𝒜 ) ∈ Θ ​ ( B ) \text{compute}(\mathcal{A})\in\Theta(B) for batch size B B . 3 3 3 Hardware optimizations might violate this linear relationship; for the present discussion the linear scaling suffices. This is the cost appearing in Equation 1 . JTL may require additional memory or data-access machinery to implement replay, but those are outside the optimization budget studied here. Our experiments match this budget by design, using the same number of parameter updates and the same per-update batch budget for both agents.

We study JTL and ITL not as competing heuristics but as the two limiting cases of a single design axis: both are instances of the broader family of Predictive CL algorithms we formalize in Section 5 ( Definition 9 ), each corresponding to a particular assumption about how the future relates to the past.

### 3 Transfer Efficiency

We wish to quantify when a JTL model is preferable to an ITL one. To this end, we define the concept of Transfer Efficiency , which is central to our analysis.

###### Definition 2 (Transfer Efficiency) .

The Transfer Efficiency for task k k is the performance gain of a Joint-Task Learning (JTL) agent over an Independent-Task Learning (ITL) baseline: TE k := ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) . \mathrm{TE}^{k}:=\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}}).

A positive value TE k > 0 \mathrm{TE}^{k}>0 indicates positive transfer , where the JTL agent’s past experience improves its performance on task k k relative to the ITL agent; conversely, TE k < 0 \mathrm{TE}^{k}<0 indicates negative transfer . Because ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}) represents the regret of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} on task k k (up to a constant), the Transfer Efficiency can equivalently be read as the regret reduction obtained by JTL relative to ITL.

To understand the mechanisms of transfer, we split the error into two components: a stationary component , the error at equilibrium, and a transient component , the error accumulated while the algorithm is actively moving toward that equilibrium. 4 4 4 We use equilibrium , stationary distribution , and convergence point interchangeably throughout the paper. These terms originate in different communities (statistical physics, Markov chains, and optimization respectively), but for the iterate sequence { θ n } \{\theta_{n}\} they all refer to the same object: the limiting distribution Q ∞ Q_{\infty} to which Q n Q_{n} converges as the algorithm runs on a fixed task. We avoid critical point , which in optimization denotes a zero-gradient parameter value rather than a distribution over parameters. Equilibrium is only well-defined if the optimization process converges , which we henceforth assume.

We denote by Q ∞ 𝒜 Q_{\infty}^{\mathcal{A}} the stationary parameter distribution to which Q n Q_{n} converges under algorithm 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , given a sufficiently long task duration. It is dictated jointly by the task data and the agent’s initialization at the task boundary. For the JTL and ITL algorithms we write Q ∞ J ​ T ​ L Q_{\infty}^{JTL} and Q ∞ I ​ T ​ L Q_{\infty}^{ITL} respectively. We also use ℒ k ​ ( Q ) := 𝔼 θ ∼ Q ​ [ ℒ k ​ ( θ ) ] \mathcal{L}^{k}(Q):=\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q}[\mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta)] to lift the expected loss to a distribution over parameters.

The Instability of a task k k ( ℐ k \mathcal{I}^{k} ) is the performance gap between the JTL and ITL agent measured at convergence ℐ k := ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ J ​ T ​ L ) − ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ I ​ T ​ L ) . \mathcal{I}^{k}:=\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{JTL})-\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{ITL}).

#### 3.1 The Critical Task Duration

The decomposition in Lemma 5 yields a first general result, holding under only minimal assumptions: when the stationary JTL solution is worse than the stationary ITL solution, there is a threshold task duration beyond which the initial advantage of transfer is lost.

### 4 Quadratic-Loss Analysis

This section makes the transfer mechanisms analytically transparent. We do two things. First, for quadratic losses we derive exact closed-form expressions for the Instability and the Transient Error—and hence for Transfer Efficiency—that expose how each quantity depends on the design choices (step size η \eta , batch size B B , parametrization). Second, we pass to an asymptotic (large- k k ) analysis that collapses these expressions into a single sufficient condition for positive transfer, governed by one environment-level descriptor: the rate at which the environment expands with new tasks. Different environments yield different balances between ITL and JTL, and this descriptor predicts which one wins.

The quadratic setting has been extensively studied in both the stochastic optimization and multi-task learning literatures, allowing us to apply existing results to our specific case. Even in such a simple setting, the interplay of learning dynamics and bias yields multiple layers of complexity, which offer powerful insights into the nature of transfer. The decomposition in Lemmas 5 and 6 is general under task-wise convergence; the closed-form expressions below additionally use standard assumptions detailed in Section A.2 (aligned positive-definite Hessians, small constant step sizes, locally stationary gradient-noise covariances, uniform observation-noise floors). These assumptions are not needed for the definitions themselves, but they make the transfer mechanisms analytically transparent.

#### 4.3 Asymptotic Conditions for Positive Transfer

The exact formulas above hold at every k k but are opaque. To expose how transfer depends on the environment, we now take an asymptotic (large- k k ) view, in which the entire ITL/JTL balance is governed by a single environment-level descriptor: the rate at which the environment expands with new tasks. Different environments yield different descriptors, and hence different balances between ITL and JTL. We call this descriptor the Growth Rate .

###### Definition 7 (Growth Rate) .

Let ψ : ℕ → ℝ + \psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+} be a monotonically increasing function such that the expected squared distance of the k k -th task from the origin scales as 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ θ ⋆ k ‖ 2 ] ∈ Θ ⁡ ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) ) . \mathbb{E}[\|\theta_{\star}^{k}\|^{2}]\in\Theta(\psi(k)).

If ψ ⁡ ( k ) = k 2 \psi(k)=k^{2} the environment has a linear drift : tasks move with constant velocity ∂ k θ ⋆ k = v \partial_{k}\theta_{\star}^{k}=v . Between these extremes, a diffusive environment ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) = k \psi(k)=k ) corresponds to tasks drifting via a random walk. Figure 6 visualizes these three cases.

The three rates have a clear interpretation. The sufficient condition in Theorem 8 reads exactly this trade-off: transfer is positive when the JTL stationary penalty is paid back within the budget the ITL agent needs to warm up. From this we rediscover the conventional wisdom that joint-task training is most valuable in the “ few-shot ” regime — where N k N_{k} is small and ITL has no time to recover from a cold start — and ceases to help when the environment is so unpredictable that the bias cost dominates.

### 5 Generalizing CL beyond the JTL/ITL dichotomy

#### 5.1 From implicit to explicit future models

We have shown, empirically and theoretically, that neither the JTL nor the ITL algorithm is universally optimal: each minimizes the Average Lifelong Error ( Equation 1 ) only under specific environmental regimes. This raises the natural question: is there a single optimal objective for continual learning, one that provably minimizes the ALE?

Foundational results in online convex optimization ( Besbes et al., 2015 ) established that, when the environment is unpredictable (i.e. adversarial), no first-order stochastic method can outperform online gradient descent ( Zinkevich, 2003 ) . However, Rakhlin and Sridharan (2013) tell us that much more can be hoped for when the future is somewhat predictable . If 𝒫 n + 1 \mathcal{P}_{n+1} were known, we could choose 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} to match it and minimize the next-step loss directly; 𝒫 n + 1 \mathcal{P}_{n+1} is not known in general, but one can always approximate the future based on some assumptions.

This lets us reinterpret the sampling choice (the choice of 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} ), and more generally the choice of objective, as an implicit assumption about the future. For JTL, the assumption is that the environment is ergodic and the future will resemble the past — the classical i.i.d. premise underlying empirical risk minimization . For ITL, the assumption is the opposite: that no structure can be reliably extrapolated, in which case online gradient descent on the current task is minimax-optimal ( Zinkevich, 2003 ; Besbes et al., 2015 ) .

Algorithms that maximize average accuracy or minimize forgetting can be viewed as implicit (or, for replay-based methods, explicit) approximations of the JTL objective. Thus the proposed re-reading opens a new way to analyze and to build CL algorithms. More importantly, the same framing suggests a constructive path: rather than fix an objective implicitly, one can make the future model an explicit design choice. We call the resulting family Predictive CL algorithms .

###### Definition 9 (Predictive CL Algorithm) .

Let ℋ < k \mathcal{H}_{<k} denote the observable history up to task k k , including past data, environments, and learning trajectories. A prediction mapping Φ \Phi maps this history to a predicted target distribution for the current task, 𝒫 ~ k = Φ ⁡ ( ℋ < k ) \tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{k}=\Phi(\mathcal{H}_{<k}) . A Predictive CL algorithm optimizes its parameters by minimizing the expected loss under the predicted distribution 𝒫 ~ k \tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{k} : inf 𝒜 1 N k ∑ n = a k b k 𝔼 θ n ∼ 𝒜 ( ⋅ ∣ H n ) 𝔼 o ∼ 𝒫 ~ k [ ℓ ( θ n , o ) ] s.t. computational constraints \displaystyle\inf_{\mathcal{A}}\,\,\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=a_{k}}^{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{n}\sim\mathcal{A}(\cdot\mid H_{n})\,}\mathbb{E}_{o\sim\tilde{\mathcal{P}}^{k}}\,\left[\ell(\theta_{n},o)\right]\qquad\text{s.t. computational constraints} (6)

Definition 9 generalizes existing CL objectives by making the future model explicit. JTL is recovered by choosing Φ ⁡ ( ℋ < k ) = ∑ j ≤ k w j k ​ 𝒫 j \Phi(\mathcal{H}_{<k})=\sum_{j\leq k}w_{j}^{k}\,\mathcal{P}^{j} (the empirical past is the prediction); ITL is recovered by Φ ⁡ ( ℋ < k ) = 𝒫 k \Phi(\mathcal{H}_{<k})=\mathcal{P}^{k} (the current task is the prediction). The Window algorithm introduced below sits between these limits. Crucially, Φ \Phi need not be fixed: it can itself be a learned model of the future.

#### 5.2 Proof of Concept: the Window Predictive CL Algorithm

Consider again the quadratic losses case of Section 4 . We now instantiate, as an example, a Predictive CL algorithm which we call the Window (W) algorithm. The Window algorithm predicts the future using a finite recent history: it is defined by a hyper-parameter W W , which we call window size . For each task k k the SGD sampling distribution 𝒟 W k \mathcal{D}^{k}_{W} is a mix of the past W W tasks 𝒟 W k = ∑ j = k − W + 1 k w j k ​ 𝒫 j . \mathcal{D}^{k}_{W}=\sum_{j=k-W+1}^{k}w_{j}^{k}\,\mathcal{P}^{j}. As W W grows the window agent converges to the JTL agent - which can be seen as an infinite window agent-, whereas for W = 1 W=1 it coincides with an ITL agent with warm starts .

In contrast to the JTL agent, whose bias is determined by the global growth of the task solutions relative to the origin, the Window agent’s bias depends on the local variations of the environment over the horizon W W . In order to capture this bias asymptotically we need to introduce a new measure, akin to the Growth Rate, which we call the Mean Squared Displacement .

###### Beyond the Window: richer future models.

The Window algorithm is a deliberately simple instance of Definition 9 : it encodes the prior that recent tasks are the most informative about the next one, summarized by a single scalar W W . The same framework accommodates far richer prediction mappings Φ \Phi —for instance, a generative model that produces samples resembling future tasks, or a learned sufficient summary of the history tailored to next-task prediction. In both cases the design question becomes which statistics of the past predict the future , and the bias-variance trade-off our framework formalizes provides the criterion under which competing Φ \Phi can be compared. We leave the exploration of such mappings to future work.

### 6 Interdisciplinary Literature Review

One of the aims of this work is to draw connections between the continual learning problem and the adjacent literatures that have developed conceptual structures and theoretical results bearing on its core questions. Our contribution is two-fold: providing CL with new analytical machinery, and offering existing fields fresh problems where their results can be tested and applied.

###### Online Learning and Stochastic Optimization.

Our analysis sits at the intersection of Online Convex Optimization (OCO) and Stochastic Optimization ( Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi, 2006 ; Hazan, 2016 ; Orabona, 2019 ) . The Average Lifelong Error metric is a finite-horizon analogue of the OCO static regret ( Zinkevich, 2003 ) , and across multiple tasks the average ALE corresponds to a notion of dynamic regret ( Herbster and Warmuth, 1998 ; Besbes et al., 2015 ) . Besbes et al. (2015) establish a minimax dynamic regret bound of O ⁡ ( T 2 / 3 ​ V T 1 / 3 ) O(T^{2/3}V_{T}^{1/3}) that grows with the comparator’s path variation V T V_{T} , formalizing how non-stationarity inflates regret with time. The role played there by the path variation V T V_{T} is played in our task-indexed setting by the Growth Rate ψ ⁡ ( k ) \psi(k) : both quantify how non-stationarity accumulates as the environment expands. Our Critical Task Duration N max ∞ N_{\max}^{\infty} then converts this descriptor into a horizon on the task-duration axis: it characterizes the point beyond which the environment’s Growth Rate ψ ⁡ ( k ) \psi(k) makes accumulating past observations actively harmful relative to learning each task afresh.

Our transient analysis of the JTL and ITL agents recovers and extends classical Lyapunov-type results on constant step-size SGD ( Murata, 1999 ; Bach and Moulines, 2013 ; Mandt et al., 2017 ) ; the Transient Potential Law (Theorem 25 ) makes the geometric role of the relative-curvature matrix ℛ k \mathcal{R}^{k} in those bounds explicit.

A central conceptual import from this literature is the framework of predictable sequences ( Rakhlin and Sridharan, 2013 ) : an online learner can improve over the worst-case minimax dynamic regret of Zinkevich (2003) ; Besbes et al. (2015) when given a useful hypothesis about future losses. Our generalization of CL beyond JTL ( Section 5 ) is precisely this idea translated to non-stationary task sequences. From this perspective, JTL (and similarly most of the continual learning algorithms it embodies) is not a new algorithm but a particular instantiation of predictable-sequence learning under an ergodicity hypothesis.

###### Multi-Task Learning (MTL).

The comparison of JTL and ITL aligns with a foundational question of Multi-Task Learning: does jointly training over multiple tasks benefit performance relative to learning each independently? JTL, as we use it, is best understood as an online, cumulative version of MTL: the task pool grows over time, and joint training is performed online over the accumulated history rather than offline on a fixed pool. The classical MTL literature operates in a different regime, typically a fixed task pool sampled i.i.d. and evaluated post-hoc; the underlying tension between sharing and interference is, however, identical.

Statistical foundations show that shared representations reduce sample complexity when the task family is self-contained ( Baxter, 2000 ; Maurer et al., 2016 ; Tripuraneni et al., 2020 ; Du et al., 2021 ) , but abstract away the optimization process; a separate line considers optimization-time interference and gradient conflict ( Standley et al., 2020 ; Sener and Koltun, 2018 ; Yu et al., 2020a ; Liu et al., 2023 ) . Neither line jointly tracks environment and optimization processes as they co-evolve, nor formalizes the resulting trade-off through an ALE-like metric.

###### Learning To Learn and Online Meta-Learning.

Our framework has its closest ties to Learning-to-Learn and Online Meta-Learning ( Kuzborskij and Orabona, 2017 ; Denevi et al., 2018 ) . The Online-Within-Online setting ( Alquier et al., 2017 ; Denevi et al., 2019 ; Khodak et al., 2019 ; Nazari and Khorram, 2021 ) considers regret accumulated across a sequence of tasks adapted via inner-loop optimization — formally analogous to our ALE. Denevi et al. (2019) ; Khodak et al. (2019) prove that the regret-optimal running prior is the centroid of past task minimizers; under our task-alignment assumption ( Assumption 22 ), this centroid coincides exactly with the JTL minimizer θ ⋆ J ​ T ​ L , k \theta_{\star}^{JTL,k} . The two algorithm families are therefore not merely similar in spirit but provably equivalent in their asymptotic target. The substantive difference lies in what they consume: meta-learning algorithms typically use past gradients or update statistics, whereas JTL uses the source data directly via replay, with consequences for variance and robustness beyond convex regimes.

A direct corollary of the MTL/MAML equivalence in over-parametrized networks ( Wang et al., 2021 ) is that our Instability bound for JTL also bounds the asymptotic interference cost of MAML; the transient trajectories of the two families, which are the focus of much of our analysis, remain distinct. Our Transfer Efficiency also reconciles the warm-start puzzle of Ash and Adams (2020) : warm-starting helps when Instability is small relative to the ITL Transient, and hurts when this inequality reverses.

###### Continual Learning.

We finally situate our contribution within continual learning. The defining aim of modern CL is to mitigate catastrophic forgetting ( Parisi et al., 2019 ; Hadsell et al., 2020 ; De Lange et al., 2022 ) , pursued via mechanisms as diverse as regularization, replay, parameter isolation, expert mixtures, and prompt-based adaptation. Although these methods differ substantially in implementation, they implicitly share a common target: maximize average accuracy across all tasks seen so far. In our terminology, each is an approximation of the JTL objective. The variety of CL methods reflects different practical strategies for approximating this common objective, not different objectives in themselves.

The closest theoretical neighbor of our work is the line of analysis on linear-model CL by Evron et al. (2022) ; Goldfarb et al. (2024) . Their analyses bound forgetting for sequential gradient descent on quadratic losses; we share the quadratic regime and the gradient-descent algorithm, but ask a different question — bounding real-time error (ALE) rather than retrospective forgetting — and obtain the Critical Task Duration as the central object of study. Our analysis is most naturally interpreted under the domain-incremental regime, where task identity shifts the input/output distribution while the label space is preserved; this matches both our experimental setting (CLEAR, MD5, Permuted/Shuffled CIFAR-10) and our quadratic-drift theory.

A complementary line of work, recently crystallized as the Big World Hypothesis ( Javed and Sutton, 2024 ) , holds that the world is too large for any bounded learner to model exhaustively—so CL is fundamentally a problem of tracking rather than memorization. This view has technical roots in the early work of Sutton et al. (2007) , and is empirically supported by recent work on the loss of plasticity ( Dohare et al., 2024 ; Dohare et al., 2021 ) . The ALE metric is itself a tracking objective, and our framework can be read as the optimization-theoretic instantiation of this hypothesis.

Within Online Continual Learning ( Cai et al., 2021 ; Lopez-Paz and Ranzato, 2017 ; Buzzega et al., 2020 ) , our Transfer Efficiency decomposition turns the empirical observation of Cai et al. (2021) —that learning efficacy and information retention conflict—into a quantitative trade-off mediated by the Critical Task Duration. Kumar et al. (2025) provide the information-theoretic ALE framing under which we operate; our contribution complements theirs by characterizing which implementable algorithms approach the information-theoretic ceiling and under what conditions. The framework also clarifies the relationship to non-stationary RL theory ( Lecarpentier and Rachelson, 2019 ; Lecarpentier et al., 2021 ; Khetarpal et al., 2022 ) , where similar adaptation–stability trade-offs appear in the context of value-function tracking.

### 7 Discussion and Conclusion

The Continual Learning (CL) literature has long been framed around mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This perspective implicitly assumes that the ideal lifelong learner is the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution—a model trained jointly on all data observed so far. In this work, we challenge this assumption, proposing a more general formulation of continual learning that shifts the focus from knowledge retention to real-time adaptation.

Our starting point is the Average Lifelong Error (ALE). Kumar et al. (2025) formalized the trade-offs underlying ALE through an information-theoretic lens, establishing the performance ceiling that a compute-bounded learner can hope to approach. Our framework complements this view by characterizing the optimization-time realization of the same trade-off: which implementable algorithms can approach this ceiling, and under what conditions. We show that real-time performance is governed by the interaction of two stochastic processes—one describing the environment, and the other the learner—and that implementation-level choices (the optimizer, the model, its parametrization, e.g. NTK versus μ \mu P) directly shape the balance between positive and negative transfer.

We compared the JTL agent (which assumes an ergodic and stable past) with the Independent-Task Learning (ITL) agent (which assumes a fully volatile environment) through the lens of Transfer Efficiency ( TE \mathrm{TE} ), a quantity that decomposes into two largely independent components: Instability (the stationary bias from optimizing over conflicting past tasks) and Transient Error (the cost of learning from a naive initialization). Under mild convergence conditions and whenever the average Instability is positive, this decomposition yields a Critical Task Duration ( N max ∞ N_{\max}^{\infty} ): a threshold beyond which the warm-start advantage of JTL is overtaken by the ITL agent’s ability to fit the current environment.

In the quadratic regime, this decomposition admits closed-form expressions that make the tension between stationary bias and the cost of learning fully transparent. Two takeaways carry over to general non-convex settings: (1) the environment’s Growth Rate —the speed at which it expands—is the decisive parameter for the balance between positive and negative transfer; and (2) once the number of tasks is sufficiently large, transfer is primarily determined by the relative weight of the Instability and the ITL Transient, with the JTL Transient acting as a lower-order correction. We empirically verified that these mechanisms persist on continual image-classification benchmarks (CLEAR and MD5) and the reinforcement-learning benchmark MT10.

Recognizing the JTL algorithm as a specific instance of predictive online learning—which rests on the assumption of predictable sequences—suggests a natural generalization: a broader family of Predictive CL algorithms that optimize explicit, environment-specific predictions of the future . As a proof of concept, we introduced the Window Algorithm, which adapts its memory horizon based on the environment’s Mean Squared Displacement (MSD), a local, interval-based analogue of the Growth Rate. In controlled drift settings, this simple approach consistently outperforms both JTL and ITL. More broadly, because the prediction mapping itself can be learned, future algorithms need not commit to a fixed objective a priori, but could instead infer the most appropriate predictive target directly from experience. We believe this represents a promising new direction for continual learning, introducing prediction as an additional axis for designing adaptive learning algorithms.

The broader contribution of this work, however, is conceptual. The framework we develop— Transfer Efficiency, Instability, and Transient Error —together with the Predictive-CL perspective, helps unify continual learning with closely related areas such as online learning, stochastic optimization, multi-task learning, and meta-learning. Beyond introducing new metrics, our framework gives the continual learning community a common language and a unified analytical toolkit for studying real-time adaptation under non-stationary environments.

###### Limitations.

Our conclusions depend on the choice of evaluation objective. ALE captures a natural notion of real-time adaptation under computational constraints, but alternative desiderata such as robustness, coverage, fairness, or worst-case performance may favor different learning strategies and retention mechanisms. Accordingly, we do not claim that ALE is the universally correct objective for continual learning. Rather, our goal is to characterize the behavior and predictions that follow from this particular formulation. Under alternative objectives, the relative performance of JTL, ITL, and other Predictive CL algorithms may differ. The proposed Predictive CL framework remains applicable as a general decomposition and analysis tool, but quantities such as the Critical Task Duration and Transfer Efficiency should be interpreted as properties of the ALE objective rather than universal characteristics of continual learning.

#### A.2 Analysis Setup and Formalism

We consider a sequence of supervised learning tasks where an agent interacts with a piecewise stationary environment, assuming task boundaries are known. We specialize the general lifelong learning framework to the case of stochastic gradient-based optimization.

###### Task-Based Formulation.

We assume the data-generating process is locally stationary , defined formally as follows:

###### Definition (Task-Based Data Generating Process) .

A history H T H_{T} is a task-based sequence if the sampling distribution 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} is locally stationary. The timeline is partitioned into intervals called tasks S k = [ a k , b k ] S_{k}=[a_{k},b_{k}] . At each time step n ∈ S k n\in S_{k} , an observation o n o_{n} is sampled from a fixed, task-specific distribution 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}^{k} .

###### Batched Observations.

For generality, we assume each observation o t o_{t} is a batch of B ≥ 1 B\geq 1 samples: o t = { ( x b , y b ) } b = 1 B . o_{t}=\{(x_{b},y_{b})\}_{b=1}^{B}. Accordingly, the full history is H t = { o 1 , … , o t } H_{t}=\{o_{1},\dots,o_{t}\} . The model f f operates at the sample level , producing predictions f ⁡ ( x b ) f(x_{b}) for each input in the batch. The batch loss is defined as the average of the per-sample losses.

###### The Data-Generating Process.

Unless stated otherwise, we assume a task-based sequence with known task switch points . We denote the task identity at time n n by z ⁡ ( n ) z(n) and the duration of task k k by N k = | S k | N_{k}=|S_{k}| . At each step n ∈ S k n\in S_{k} , a batch o n o_{n} is sampled from 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}^{k} . The expected loss for task k k over the model parameters θ \theta is: ℒ k ​ ( θ ) := 𝔼 ( x , y ) ∼ 𝒫 k ​ [ ℓ ⁡ ( θ , ( x , y ) ) ] . \mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}^{k}}[\ell(\theta,(x,y))]. (11) The corresponding oracle minimizer is θ ⋆ k = arg ⁡ min θ ​ ℒ k ​ ( θ ) \theta^{k}_{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta) .

###### Measures of Error and Performance.

Within this formalism, we define the Average Task Error ( ALE k \mathrm{ALE}^{k} ) as the task-specific view of the lifelong ALE: ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ) := 1 N k ​ ∑ n = a k b k 𝔼 θ ∼ Q n ​ [ ℒ k ​ ( θ ) ] . \mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}):=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n=a_{k}}^{b_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim Q_{n}}[\mathcal{L}^{k}(\theta)]. (12) It follows that ALE ⁡ ( 𝒜 ) = ∑ k = 1 K w ¯ k ⋅ ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mathrm{ALE}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bar{w}_{k}\cdot\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}) with task weights w ¯ k = N k / T \bar{w}_{k}=N_{k}/T .

The Transfer Efficiency (TE) for task k k quantifies the performance gain of a Joint-Task Learning (JTL) agent over an Independent-Task Learning (ITL) baseline: TE k := ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) . \mathrm{TE}^{k}:=\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}}). (13)

##### A.2.1 The Algorithm as a Markov Process

We view the learning algorithm 𝒜 \mathcal{A} as a discrete-time stochastic process { θ n } n ≥ 0 \{\theta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0} taking values in the parameter space Θ ⊆ ℝ d \Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} . Parameter evolution is governed by a stochastic update rule U U . At step n n , the update depends on the current parameters θ n \theta_{n} and a data batch ξ n \xi_{n} drawn from an algorithm-specific sampling distribution 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} : θ n + 1 = U ⁡ ( θ n , ξ n ) , where ​ ξ n ∼ 𝒟 n . \theta_{n+1}=U(\theta_{n},\xi_{n}),\quad\text{where }\xi_{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}. (14) Crucially, the optimization distribution 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} is distinct from the environmental distribution 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} . While 𝒫 n \mathcal{P}_{n} dictates the incoming data stream, 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} determines the data used for the update. For replay-based algorithms (such as our JTL agent), 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} may incorporate data from previous tasks.

Assuming the sampling strategy is consistent within task boundaries ( 𝒟 n = 𝒟 k \mathcal{D}_{n}=\mathcal{D}^{k} for all n ∈ S k n\in S_{k} ), the process induces a time-homogeneous Markov Transition Operator 𝒯 k \mathcal{T}^{k} . For any bounded measurable test function ϕ : Θ → ℝ \phi:\Theta\to\mathbb{R} , the operator is defined as: ( 𝒯 k ​ ϕ ) ​ ( θ ) := 𝔼 ξ ∼ 𝒟 k ​ [ ϕ ⁡ ( U ⁡ ( θ , ξ ) ) ] . (\mathcal{T}^{k}\phi)(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}[\phi(U(\theta,\xi))]. (15) The sequence of parameter distributions { Q n } n ∈ S k \{Q_{n}\}_{n\in S_{k}} evolves according to this linear operator, Q n + 1 = Q n ​ 𝒯 k Q_{n+1}=Q_{n}\mathcal{T}^{k} (following the Markov chain convention where measures act as row vectors).

###### Convergence.

A central aspect of our analysis is characterizing the long-term behavior of the learning algorithm. An algorithm is said to converge if its transition operator 𝒯 k \mathcal{T}^{k} admits a stationary distribution Q ∞ Q_{\infty} ; if run indefinitely, the process will converge to this distribution. This property allows us to rigorously decouple the effect of starting conditions (the transient phase) from asymptotic performance (the stationary phase).

###### Stochastic Gradient Descent Instantiations.

We analyze two canonical instances of SGD with a constant step-size η \eta . Both employ the standard gradient step θ n + 1 = θ n − η ∇ ℓ ( θ n , ξ n ) \theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\eta\nabla\ell(\theta_{n},\xi_{n}) , differing only in their sampling distributions 𝒟 k \mathcal{D}^{k} and task-boundary initialization strategies.

###### ITL SGD ( 𝒜 ITL \mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}} ).

The ITL agent optimizes solely on the current data stream. Its sampling distribution matches the current task distribution ( 𝒟 ITL k = 𝒫 k \mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{ITL}}=\mathcal{P}^{k} ). At task boundaries, it resets its parameters to a fixed initialization distribution Q init Q_{\text{init}} , discarding prior knowledge to prevent negative transfer.

###### JTL SGD ( 𝒜 JTL \mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}} ).

The JTL agent minimizes the average loss across all seen tasks. To maintain time-homogeneous dynamics within a task, we model experience replay as a fixed mixture approximation . The sampling distribution 𝒟 JTL k \mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{JTL}} is a weighted mixture of the current and preceding task distributions: 𝒟 JTL k := ∑ j = 1 k w j k ​ 𝒫 j , where ​ w j k = N j ∑ i = 1 k N i . \mathcal{D}^{k}_{\text{JTL}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w^{k}_{j}\,\mathcal{P}^{j},\quad\text{where }w^{k}_{j}=\frac{N_{j}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}N_{i}}. (16) Unlike the ITL agent, the JTL agent inherits parameters from the previous task ( θ a k = θ b k − 1 \theta_{a_{k}}=\theta_{b_{k-1}} ), enabling forward transfer.

##### A.2.2 Why use reset ITL as the reference?

The ITL baseline used throughout the main comparison is intentionally reset at task boundaries. This choice isolates the value of the JTL objective as a prediction of the future : JTL assumes that the accumulated past remains predictive, whereas reset ITL assumes no such structure and starts each task from the same prior. The comparison therefore measures the benefit and cost of injecting historical bias into the optimization process.

A warm-start current-task learner is also natural: it would set 𝒟 k = 𝒫 k \mathcal{D}^{k}=\mathcal{P}^{k} as ITL does, but initialize task k k from the previous endpoint rather than from Q init Q_{\mathrm{init}} . In our decomposition, this variant has the same stationary distribution as ITL whenever the current-task optimizer is task-wise convergent, because both optimize only 𝒫 k \mathcal{P}^{k} . Thus its stationary gap against JTL is still the same instability term ℐ k \mathcal{I}^{k} . What changes is only the transient term: TE warm k = − ℐ k + δ k ​ ( 𝒜 warm ) − δ k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) . \mathrm{TE}^{k}_{\mathrm{warm}}=-\mathcal{I}^{k}+\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{warm}})-\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{JTL}}). Consequently, warm starts can shift the critical duration by reducing the current-task transient cost, but they do not remove the central trade-off: retaining past information helps only when the transient advantage it provides exceeds the stationary bias induced by optimizing against outdated tasks. The Window agent in Section 5 includes this case as W = 1 W=1 , making it a useful bridge between reset ITL, warm-start adaptation, and full JTL.

#### A.3 Decomposition: Instability and Transient Error

To rigorously analyze the source of the performance gap between ITL and JTL agents, we introduce a theoretical decomposition based on the asymptotic behavior of the learning process.

###### Definition 12 (Task-wise Convergence) .

An algorithm 𝒜 \mathcal{A} is Task-wise Convergent if, for any task k k , the associated Markov operator 𝒯 k \mathcal{T}^{k} admits a stationary probability measure Q ∞ k Q_{\infty}^{k} . Formally, if the task duration were infinite, the distribution of iterates Q n k Q^{k}_{n} would converge to Q ∞ k Q_{\infty}^{k} regardless of initialization: lim n → ∞ W 2 ​ ( Q n k , Q ∞ k ) = 0 , \lim_{n\to\infty}W_{2}(Q^{k}_{n},Q_{\infty}^{k})=0, (17) where W 2 W_{2} is the Wasserstein-2 distance ( Villani, 2009 ) , standard in the convergence analysis of stochastic optimization algorithms.

If convergence holds, the Average Task Error ( ALE k \mathrm{ALE}^{k} ) can be split into two components: the stationary component (error at equilibrium) and the transient component (error accumulated while moving toward equilibrium).

###### Definition 13 (Instability ℐ k \mathcal{I}^{k} ) .

Let 𝒜 ITL \mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}} and 𝒜 JTL \mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}} be task-wise convergent algorithms with stationary distributions Q ∞ ITL Q_{\infty}^{\text{ITL}} and Q ∞ JTL Q_{\infty}^{\text{JTL}} . The Instability measures the performance gap in the asymptotic limit: ℐ k := ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ JTL ) − ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ ITL ) . \mathcal{I}^{k}:=\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{\text{JTL}})-\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{\text{ITL}}). (18)

###### Definition 14 (Transient Error δ k \delta^{k} ) .

For a task-wise convergent algorithm 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , the Transient Error captures the excess loss accumulated during the mixing phase. It is defined as the average deviation from the stationary risk over the task duration S k S_{k} : δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) := 1 N k ​ ∑ n ∈ S k ( ℒ k ​ ( Q n ) − ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ 𝒜 ) ) . \delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}):=\frac{1}{N_{k}}\sum_{n\in S_{k}}\left(\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{\mathcal{A}})\right). (19)

This yields a rigorous decomposition of Transfer Efficiency, allowing us to treat the “quality of the final solution” and the “speed of learning” as distinct analytical objects.

###### Lemma 15 (Exact TE Decomposition) .

For any task k k , assuming the algorithms are task-wise convergent, the Transfer Efficiency decomposes exactly as: TE k = − ℐ k ⏟ Stationary Gap + ( δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − δ k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) ) ⏟ Learning Speed Gap . \mathrm{TE}^{k}=\underbrace{-\mathcal{I}^{k}}_{\text{Stationary Gap}}+\underbrace{\left(\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}})\right)}_{\text{Learning Speed Gap}}. (20)

###### Proof.

By definition, ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ) = ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ 𝒜 ) + δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) \mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{\mathcal{A}})+\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}) . Substituting this into TE k = ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − ALE k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) \mathrm{TE}^{k}=\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\mathrm{ALE}^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}}) and rearranging terms yields the result. ∎

When tasks have equal duration N N , we can state a general condition for when transfer is beneficial. To do so, we distinguish the average (per-step) Transient Error δ k \delta^{k} from the cumulative Transient Error, defined as Δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) := N ⋅ δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) = ∑ n ∈ S k ( ℒ k ​ ( Q n ) − ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ 𝒜 ) ) \Delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}):=N\cdot\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{n\in S_{k}}(\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}^{\mathcal{A}})) .

###### Lemma 16 (The Critical Task Duration) .

Assume a sequence of K K tasks of equal duration N N . Let Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 ) = 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K Δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) \bar{\Delta}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}) denote the expected cumulative Transient Error, and ℐ ¯ = 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K ℐ k \bar{\mathcal{I}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{I}^{k} the average instability. The breakeven horizon is given by: N max ∞ = Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) ℐ ¯ . ###### Proof.

For equal-duration tasks, the average Transfer Efficiency is positive if: 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K ( δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − δ k ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) ) > 1 K ​ ∑ k = 1 K ℐ k . \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\delta^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}})\right)>\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{I}^{k}. By definition, the instability ℐ k \mathcal{I}^{k} relies on the stationary distribution and is independent of N N . Substituting δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) = Δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) / N \delta^{k}(\mathcal{A})=\Delta^{k}(\mathcal{A})/N , the condition for positive transfer becomes: Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 ITL ) − Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 JTL ) N > ℐ ¯ . \frac{\bar{\Delta}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{ITL}})-\bar{\Delta}(\mathcal{A}_{\text{JTL}})}{N}>\bar{\mathcal{I}}. If ℐ ¯ > 0 \bar{\mathcal{I}}>0 and the cumulative Transient Error is sub-linear ( Δ ¯ ​ ( 𝒜 ) ∈ o ​ ( N ) \bar{\Delta}(\mathcal{A})\in o(N) ), the left-hand transient advantage vanishes with N N , while the stationary gap remains. Solving the breakeven equality yields the critical threshold N max ∞ N_{\max}^{\infty} . If ℐ ¯ ≤ 0 \bar{\mathcal{I}}\leq 0 , the stationary component does not make JTL worse on average, so this argument does not imply an eventual detrimental-transfer horizon. ∎

###### On the Sub-Linear Transient Error Assumption.

The condition that the cumulative Transient Error grows sub-linearly ( Δ k ​ ( 𝒜 ) ∈ o ​ ( N ) \Delta^{k}(\mathcal{A})\in o(N) ) simply requires that the algorithm successfully converges toward its stationary risk, meaning the instantaneous error ℒ k ​ ( Q n ) − ℒ k ​ ( Q ∞ ) \mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{n})-\mathcal{L}^{k}(Q_{\infty}) vanishes as n → ∞ n\to\infty . This is a mild assumption across standard optimization regimes: • Convex Optimization (Linear Models): In stochastic strongly convex optimization, sub-optimality decays at 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/n) . Integrating over N N steps yields a cumulative error bounded by 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) ∈ o ⁡ ( N ) \mathcal{O}(\log N)\in o(N) . Even in strictly convex or non-smooth cases with a rate of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n}) , the cumulative error is 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ) ∈ o ⁡ ( N ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})\in o(N) ( Bubeck, 2015 ) .

• Non-Convex Optimization (Neural Networks): While deep networks optimize non-convex landscapes, they practically converge to local minima. Theoretically, under structural assumptions like the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition ( Karimi et al., 2016 ) or the Neural Tangent Kernel regime ( Jacot et al., 2018 ) , SGD can achieve linear convergence rates, decaying exponentially as 𝒪 ⁡ ( e − λ ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(e^{-\lambda n}) . In such regimes, the cumulative Transient Error is bounded by a constant 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) , trivially satisfying the o ⁡ ( N ) o(N) condition.

##### A.4.2 JTL Geometry

For JTL, deriving the instability and transient cost requires careful handling of two distinct geometries: the curvature driving the optimization dynamics (the mixture Hessian, H dyn H_{\text{dyn}} ) and the curvature determining the evaluation cost (the target task Hessian, H eval H_{\text{eval}} ).

To obtain a tractable closed-form solution that preserves geometric intuition, we rely on a task alignment assumption.

###### Assumption 22 (Task Alignment) .

We assume the Hessians of all tasks are simultaneously diagonalizable. This implies that while tasks may vary in difficulty (eigenvalues), their principal axes of curvature (eigenvectors) are aligned. Consequently, the driving and evaluation Hessians commute: H dyn ​ H eval = H eval ​ H dyn . H_{\text{dyn}}H_{\text{eval}}=H_{\text{eval}}H_{\text{dyn}}. (28)

To interpret the cost geometrically, we introduce the Relative Curvature Matrix , denoted by ℛ \mathcal{R} . This matrix measures how steep the evaluation landscape is relative to the driving landscape: ℛ := H dyn − 1 ​ H eval . \mathcal{R}:=H_{\text{dyn}}^{-1}H_{\text{eval}}. (29) The eigenvalues of ℛ \mathcal{R} provide a scaling factor for the cost. If ℛ ≺ I \mathcal{R}\prec I , the agent benefits from a pre-conditioning effect, effectively training on a steeper surrogate than the test task.

We also require the following assumption, standard in the analysis of constant step-size SGD ( Mandt et al., 2017 ; Bach and Moulines, 2013 ) .

###### Assumption 23 (Bounded Curvature) .

We assume the Hessian matrices for all tasks have eigenvalues bounded strictly away from zero and infinity. There exist constants 0 < λ min ≤ λ max 0<\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\max} such that for any task k k : λ min ​ I ⪯ H k ⪯ λ max ​ I . \lambda_{\min}I\preceq H^{k}\preceq\lambda_{\max}I. Consequently, the mixture Hessian H dyn = ∑ j = 1 k w j k ​ H j H_{\text{dyn}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}^{k}H^{j} satisfies the same spectral bounds.

This result provides a powerful physical intuition for the cost of learning: it is simply the difference between where the agent started and where it ended, measured on a quadratic potential surface warped by ℛ \mathcal{R} . • JTL: Here, ℛ ≠ I \mathcal{R}\neq I . The potential surface is stretched according to the task mixture. #### A.5 Asymptotic Dynamics of the First and Second Order Moments

In Section A.4 , we derived closed-form expressions for the instability and transient costs by taking expectations over the stochasticity of the agent’s parameter trajectory. To analyze the asymptotic stability of the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) and Independent-Task Learning (ITL) agents as the number of tasks grows, we must now account for the randomness in the environment itself. Therefore, in this section, unless otherwise specified, the expectation operator 𝔼 ⁡ [ ⋅ ] \mathbb{E}[\cdot] is taken over the task generation process (i.e., the distribution of the task sequence).

To formalize how "concentrated" or "spread out" the tasks are, we introduce the notion of a Growth Rate ψ ⁡ ( k ) \psi(k) , which characterizes the asymptotic spread of the task optima as a function of the number of tasks k k .

###### Definition 26 (Growth Rate) .

Let ψ : ℕ → ℝ + \psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+} be a monotonically increasing function such that the expected squared distance of the k k -th task optimum from the origin scales as: 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ θ ⋆ k ‖ 2 ] ∈ Θ ⁡ ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) ) . \mathbb{E}[\|\theta_{\star}^{k}\|^{2}]\in\Theta(\psi(k)). (47)

In the analysis that follows, we consider three distinct regimes of environment growth: Bounded ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) = 1 \psi(k)=1 ), Diffusive ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) = k \psi(k)=k ), and Linear ( ψ ⁡ ( k ) = k 2 \psi(k)=k^{2} ).

##### A.5.1 Geometric Drift

As the agent incorporates new tasks, the stationary targets of the mixture shift. We define the Geometric Drift of the first and second-order moments as the step-wise difference in the asymptotic JTL targets: Δ ​ μ k = θ ⋆ JTL , k − 1 − θ ⋆ JTL , k , Δ ​ Σ k = Σ ∞ JTL , k − 1 − Σ ∞ JTL , k . \displaystyle\Delta\mu^{k}=\theta_{\star}^{\text{JTL},k-1}-\theta_{\star}^{\text{JTL},k},\qquad\Delta\Sigma^{k}=\Sigma_{\infty}^{\text{JTL},k-1}-\Sigma_{\infty}^{\text{JTL},k}. (48) We proceed to characterize the asymptotic behavior of this drift depending on the growth rate function. Table 4 summarizes the convergence properties for each regime.

#### A.7 Window Algorithm as an Instance of Predictive CL

We introduce the Window algorithm as a concrete instance of our generalized CL framework: a Predictive CL algorithm that bridges both the ITL and JTL approaches through the definition of a window of tasks . At any task k k , the Window agent predicts the relevant future distribution with the recent W W tasks and optimizes the average loss over that window, where we call W W the window size . Specifically, the sampling distribution 𝒟 W k \mathcal{D}^{k}_{W} is: 𝒟 W k = ∑ j = k − W + 1 k ρ j k ​ 𝒫 j ρ j k = | N j | ∑ i ∈ [ k − W , k ] N i . \mathcal{D}^{k}_{W}=\sum_{j=k-W+1}^{k}\rho_{j}^{k}\,\mathcal{P}^{j}\qquad\rho_{j}^{k}=\frac{|N_{j}|}{\sum_{i\in[k-W,k]}N_{i}}. Like the JTL agent, the Window agent’s parameters are not reset at the beginning of a new task. Clearly, as W W grows, the Window agent converges to the JTL agent—which can be viewed as an infinite window agent—whereas setting W = 1 W=1 recovers a variant of the ITL agent with warm starts .

##### B.2.1 CLEAR

The CLEAR dataset ( Lin et al., 2021 ) is a collection of images spanning the years 2004–2014, designed to evaluate models under natural temporal distribution shifts. ##### B.2.2 MULTIDATASET (MD5)

The MULTIDATASET benchmark consists of a sequence of K = 5 K=5 distinct open-source classification datasets with no semantic overlap. Specifically, the tasks consist of classifying automobile models ( Krause et al., 2013 ) , aircraft models ( Maji et al., 2013 ) , textures ( Cimpoi et al., 2014 ) , dishes ( Bossard et al., 2014 ) , and pets ( Parkhi et al., 2012 ) . All input images are resized to 224 × \times 224 pixels and normalized using the standard ImageNet statistics ( μ = [ 0.485 , 0.456 , 0.406 ] \mu=[0.485,0.456,0.406] , σ = [ 0.229 , 0.224 , 0.225 ] \sigma=[0.229,0.224,0.225] ). ##### B.2.3 Meta-World

Meta-World ( Yu et al., 2020b ) is a suite of simulated robotic manipulation tasks built on the MuJoCo physics engine. Our experiments use the MT10 benchmark, a fixed collection of 10 10 distinct manipulation tasks ( reach , push , pick-place , door-open , drawer-open , drawer-close , button-press-topdown , peg-insert-side , window-open , window-close ) on the shared Sawyer robot. We use the v3 environment versions provided by the official Meta-World release. Unlike the meta-learning split (ML10), MT10 is designed for multi-task and continual evaluation on a common task set, with no held-out tasks.

At each step, the environment yields a 39 39 -dimensional observation vector that includes the robot’s joint positions, the gripper state, and the spatial coordinates of relevant objects, with the goal position zero-masked. For the JTL agent, this observation is concatenated with a 10 10 -dimensional one-hot task identifier, yielding a 49 49 -dimensional input. The agent emits a 4 4 -dimensional continuous action (end-effector delta plus gripper command), with components squashed to [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] via a tanh \tanh . Each episode lasts at most 500 500 time steps and yields a binary success flag in addition to the shaped reward.

#### B.4 Training Procedures & Networks

###### Supervised Learning

All supervised learning agents consist of a deep neural network backbone, an optimizer, and a learning rate scheduler. Across all supervised experiments, the core architecture is a ResNet-18 ( RN18 ). The output dimension of the final linear classification head is set to the total number of classes in the respective benchmark (100 for CLEAR, 30 for MD5, and 10 for Permuted/Shuffled CIFAR-10), and this head is shared across all tasks. Both the Independent-Task Learning (ITL) and Joint-Task Learning (JTL) agents are trained for an identical number of gradient steps h h per task. General optimization configurations are detailed in Table 8 , with explicitly tuned hyperparameters listed in Table 9 .

ITL Agent. Given a sequence of K K tasks, the ITL agent is trained to minimize the cross-entropy loss exclusively on the training data of the current active task. To establish a strict ITL baseline and prevent any forward transfer, both the network weights and the optimizer state of the ITL agent are completely re-initialized to their random starting priors at the beginning of every task.

JTL Agent. The JTL agent minimizes the expected loss over the union of all currently and previously observed tasks. In practice, this is implemented using an exact experience replay buffer: at each gradient step, the optimizer processes a concatenated batch comprising data from the current task stream alongside data uniformly sampled from the buffer of previous tasks. Unlike the ITL agent, the JTL agent’s network parameters and optimizer state are preserved across task boundaries.

###### Reinforcement Learning

In our reinforcement learning experiments, we compare ITL and JTL agents using Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) ( Haarnoja et al., 2018a ) on the MT10 subset of the Meta-World benchmark ( Yu et al., 2020b ) , with automatic entropy temperature tuning ( Haarnoja et al., 2018b ) . SAC is the standard off-policy continuous-control algorithm used in the multi-task Meta-World literature ( Yu et al., 2020b ) , and its use of a single replay buffer makes the ITL and JTL agents differ only in (i) what data populates the buffer and (ii) whether the policy is conditioned on a task identifier; the underlying optimizer, network architecture, and per-epoch compute budget are otherwise identical.

Architecture (shared). For both agents, the actor and the twin critics are MLPs with two hidden layers of 400 400 units and ReLU activations. The actor outputs the mean and (clipped) log-standard-deviation of a diagonal Gaussian, squashed by tanh \tanh ; the critics output a scalar Q Q -value. The log-std is clipped to [ − 20 , 2 ] [-20,2] .

ITL Agent. For each task in MT10 we train a separate SAC agent from a random initialization, with its own replay buffer and its own learned entropy temperature α \alpha . The agent observes only the 39-dimensional Meta-World state of its task; no task identifier is appended. Each ITL run lasts 500 500 epochs; per epoch we collect 40 40 rollouts of length 500 500 ( 2 × 10 4 2\times 10^{4} environment steps) into the buffer and then perform 500 500 SAC gradient updates with mini-batch size 500 500 .

JTL Agent. We train a single SAC agent across all MT10 tasks under a curriculum : at epoch e e the active set is the first 1 + ⌊ e / N ⌋ 1+\lfloor e/N\rfloor tasks in a fixed ordering, with task duration N = 500 N=500 epochs. The actor and critics are conditioned on a 10 10 -dimensional one-hot task identifier concatenated to the observation. A single replay buffer of capacity 10 6 10^{6} is shared across all tasks and is never reset; whenever a new task becomes active we re-initialize the Adam states of the actor and critic optimizers (the entropy temperature is kept) so that the optimizer can adapt to the new objective. Per epoch we collect 200 200 rollouts of length 500 500 ( 10 5 10^{5} environment steps) by uniformly cycling over the currently active environments, then perform 500 500 SAC gradient updates with mini-batch size 500 500 sampled uniformly from the shared buffer. Evaluation each epoch is performed on the current task.

Task ordering. For the curriculum experiments shown in the main paper we use the easy_to_hard ordering, obtained by sorting MT10 tasks in decreasing order of the final single-task success rate of the ITL SAC agents under the same configuration. The resulting sequence is drawer-close , window-close , button-press-topdown , drawer-open , reach , window-open , door-open , push , peg-insert-side , pick-place . Each configuration (ITL and JTL) is replicated over 10 10 seeds; per-task figures aggregate seeds for which the entire 500 500 -epoch stage is logged, with a minimum of 7 7 seeds per relative epoch.

##### B.4.1 Hyperparameters

###### Supervised Learning

The key hyperparameters—learning rate and weight decay—are carefully tuned separately for each combination of agent and benchmark using a grid search. To ensure a fair comparison, the optimizer and scheduler dynamics are fixed across most supervised learning experiments. We employ Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) paired with a task-wise Cosine Annealing scheduler. Specifically, the learning rate is smoothly annealed toward zero over the course of each task’s duration ( h h steps) and undergoes a hard reset to its maximum initial value at the beginning of the next task, providing the necessary plasticity to rapidly minimize the changing objective.

###### Reinforcement Learning

For a fair comparison, ITL and JTL share the same SAC core hyperparameters (network width, learning rates, γ \gamma , τ \tau , entropy auto-tuning, mini-batch size, and the number of SAC gradient updates per epoch). The two agents differ only along axes that are intrinsic to the ITL/JTL distinction: (i) the size of the rollout collected per epoch ( 2 × 10 4 2\times 10^{4} environment steps for ITL on its single active task vs. 10 5 10^{5} for JTL split uniformly over the currently active tasks), (ii) the presence of a one-hot task conditioning for JTL, (iii) replay buffer scope (per-task vs. shared across the curriculum), and (iv) optimizer state at task boundaries (full re-initialization at every task for ITL vs. Adam-state reset of actor/critic only when a new task becomes active for JTL). All optimization is performed with Adam. Here compute-matched refers to the gradient-update budget—the number of SAC updates ( 500 500 ) and the mini-batch size ( 500 500 ) per epoch are identical for the two agents—rather than the environment-step count, which differs by construction ( 2 × 10 4 2\times 10^{4} steps per epoch for ITL on its single task vs. 10 5 10^{5} for JTL split over the active tasks); if anything, JTL is given more environment data per epoch, yet still underperforms.

We follow the MT-SAC configuration of Yu et al. (2020b) for the shared core, with the ITL-side cycle/rollout schedule taken from the single-task SAC appendix of the same work. The resulting hyperparameters are reported in Table 10 ; per-task and per-curriculum runtime budgets follow directly from the rollout schedule and the number of gradient updates.

Effect of the SAC mini-batch size on ITL agents. We fix the SAC mini-batch size to 500 500 for both ITL and JTL so that the two agents share an identical per-update compute budget ( Table 10 ). Since the quadratic analysis predicts that the optimization-induced ( Variance ) penalty scales as 1 / B 1/B in the batch size B B ( Section 4 ), it is natural to ask whether the absolute performance of the agents, and hence the task-difficulty ordering that drives the aggregate Transfer Efficiency, is sensitive to this choice. We conducted a batch-size ablation for the single-task (ITL) SAC agents, comparing B = 500 B=500 against a larger B = 5000 B=5000 under otherwise identical hyperparameters. Each of the 10 10 ITL agents is trained for 500 500 epochs ( 2 × 10 4 2\times 10^{4} environment steps per epoch, i.e. 10 7 10^{7} steps per task) in the easy_to_hard ordering; we compared the mean success rate over 10 10 seeds with a ± \pm SEM band, smoothed with a centered rolling average. Two observations stood out. First, the larger batch size yields a uniformly higher and lower-variance success rate on nearly every task, consistent with the 1 / B 1/B gradient-noise scaling: the reduced stochasticity in each SAC update translates into faster and more stable convergence (most visibly on the harder push , door-open , button-press-topdown , and window-open blocks). Second, and more importantly for our claims, the relative ordering of the tasks by difficulty is essentially unchanged: the tasks that are easy at B = 500 B=500 (e.g. drawer-close , reach , window-close ) remain easy at B = 5000 B=5000 , and the tasks that single-task SAC fails to solve within the budget (e.g. pick-place , peg-insert-side ) remain unsolved at both batch sizes. Because the per-task Transfer Efficiency decomposition is driven by this difficulty ordering and by the high-instability tasks, the qualitative conclusion—negative aggregate TE on MT10 dominated by a handful of high-instability tasks—is robust to the mini-batch size. We therefore report the compute-matched B = 500 B=500 setting for all quantitative results in the main text; the per-task trajectory illustration in Figure 4 instead shows the visually cleaner B = 5000 B=5000 runs, whose task-difficulty ordering and hence the qualitative transfer story is identical.

#### B.5 Additional Figures and Empirical Substantiation

This subsection collects supporting diagnostics for the empirical claims in the main text. The figures should be read as mechanism checks for the Transfer Efficiency (TE) decomposition rather than as separate benchmark claims: they expose task heterogeneity, instability proxies, width- and parametrization-dependent optimization bias, and the real-time trajectories that are averaged into the final TE curves.

##### B.5.1 Pairwise Transfer is Asymmetric, and Why Ordering Matters

To isolate the directional structure of transfer, we measure pairwise interference directly: for every ordered pair ( t i , t j ) (t_{i},t_{j}) of MT10 tasks we train a two-task joint (JTL) agent and compare the performance it achieves on t i t_{i} against the single-task (ITL) specialist for t i t_{i} . Figure 21 reports the resulting normalized gap Δ ⁡ ( t i ∣ t j ) = ITL ​ ( t i ) − JTL ​ ( t i ∣ t j ) ITL ​ ( t i ) , \Delta(t_{i}\mid t_{j})\;=\;\frac{\text{ITL}(t_{i})-\text{JTL}(t_{i}\mid t_{j})}{\text{ITL}(t_{i})}, where the row indexes the evaluated task t i t_{i} and the column indexes the co-trained task t j t_{j} . With the figure’s sign convention, Δ > 0 \Delta>0 (red) means joint training (JTL) hurts the row task relative to its ITL specialist (ITL is better), Δ < 0 \Delta<0 (blue) means JTL helps it (JTL is better), and the diagonal is 0 0 by construction.

The salient structural fact is that the matrix is not symmetric : in general Δ ⁡ ( t i ∣ t j ) ≠ Δ ⁡ ( t j ∣ t i ) \Delta(t_{i}\mid t_{j})\neq\Delta(t_{j}\mid t_{i}) . Transfer between two tasks is directional—one task can be substantially harmed by a partner that it, in turn, helps. Drawer-close and peg-insert-side , for instance, are improved by joint training with almost every partner (predominantly blue rows), whereas push , pick-place , and button-press-topdown are degraded by most partners (predominantly red rows), and the two directions of a given pair frequently carry opposite signs.

This asymmetry has a direct consequence for the lifelong objective. Because the quantity we ultimately optimize is the time-averaged error across the whole task sequence , a pair in which one direction is positive and the other negative need not produce net-negative transfer: the gains on the helped task can offset or outweigh the losses on the harmed task. More importantly, asymmetry turns task ordering into a usable degree of freedom. Concretely, suppose Δ ⁡ ( t 1 ∣ t 2 ) > 0 \Delta(t_{1}\mid t_{2})>0 (joint training would hurt t 1 t_{1} ) while Δ ⁡ ( t 2 ∣ t 1 ) < 0 \Delta(t_{2}\mid t_{1})<0 (joint training helps t 2 t_{2} ). Then presenting the tasks in the order t 1 → t 2 t_{1}\rightarrow t_{2} (training t 1 t_{1} alone first, and only introducing t 2 t_{2} afterwards under joint training) lets t 1 t_{1} bank its ITL-level performance during its first phase while t 2 t_{2} reaps the positive transfer during the joint phase. The lifelong error over the pair can then fall below the average of the two ITL specialists, even though the symmetric (simultaneous, from-scratch) joint agent would have shown mixed or negative transfer. In other words, the off-diagonal sign structure of Figure 21 is precisely the lever that the curriculum exploits, and it is the structural reason to adopt an explicit ordering.

We stress that the matrix is measured under simultaneous two-task training from a shared initialization, so it is a proxy for the directional interference. It motivates ordering as a mechanism and explains the observed sensitivity of aggregate TE to the task sequence. A systematic search over orderings that maximize lifelong performance using this asymmetry is a natural direction left to future work.

##### B.5.2 Proxy measures of instability

We consider two proxies to measure Instability. The first, ℐ C ​ U \mathcal{I}_{CU} , approximates the theoretical definition ( Definition 3 ) by training ITL and JTL models to convergence and measuring the difference in their average task-wise error. The second, ℐ t ​ r ​ a ​ n ​ s ​ f ​ e ​ r \mathcal{I}_{transfer} , is a heuristic that measures the average off-diagonal error in a task transfer matrix (i.e., the error of a model trained on task i i when evaluated on task j j ). In practice, ℐ t ​ r ​ a ​ n ​ s ​ f ​ e ​ r \mathcal{I}_{transfer} is computationally cheaper.

As shown in Table 11 , both proxies confirm our qualitative assessment: CLEAR exhibits low Instability, while MD5 and MT10 are high. The proxies also validate our methodology for the controlled benchmarks, showing that Instability increases with the permutation size (PC-16 vs. PC-32) and the label shuffle percentage (SC-10% to SC-50%). The two proxy columns are not expected to match numerically: ℐ C ​ U \mathcal{I}_{CU} compares the converged JTL and ITL solutions directly, while ℐ t ​ r ​ a ​ n ​ s ​ f ​ e ​ r \mathcal{I}_{transfer} measures off-diagonal transfer between separately trained task specialists. Their agreement at the level of ordering is the relevant point for our empirical use of Instability.

##### B.5.3 Scaling Deep Linear Networks Experiments

Figures 22 and 23 expand the deep-linear study by varying hidden width while holding the data-generating process fixed. These experiments isolate a key message from the optimization-bias analysis: even when the realizable input-output map is linear, the training parametrization changes the TE landscape. Increasing width does not simply make JTL and ITL equivalent. Instead, the lazy and rich regimes trace different families of TE frontiers because they induce different optimization paths, different effective conditioning, and different amounts of Instability after joint training.

The lazy-regime simulations in Figure 22 behave most like the fixed-feature or neural-tangent picture. Width increases capacity and smooths some finite-width effects, but the qualitative transfer boundary remains governed by the same tradeoff as in the shallow theory: JTL reduces transient optimization cost by reusing past data, while it pays an Instability cost when the current task has drifted too far from the historical average. Thus positive TE persists only in regions where the transient savings dominate the mismatch induced by jointly fitting previous tasks.

The rich-regime simulations in Figure 23 show that feature learning changes this balance. Because the representation can move during training, the model is not constrained to interpolate between tasks in the same way as in the lazy regime. This can improve transfer when the tasks share useful structure, but it can also amplify path dependence when the representation learned for earlier tasks is poorly aligned with later ones. The comparison between the two grids therefore supports the claim that TE is not determined only by task drift and sample count; it also depends on the optimizer-induced solution selected within an overparameterized model class.

##### B.5.4 Real-time training trajectories

The main text reports TE after averaging over tasks and seeds. The trajectories below show where those averages come from in time. In particular, they distinguish transient effects at the beginning of each task from persistent bias or interference accumulated across the sequence.

Figure 25 is the trajectory-level analogue of the continuous-drift phase diagrams. At small drift, the history remains predictive of the current optimum, so JTL starts each task closer to a useful solution and the advantage is mostly positive. As drift increases, the same history becomes stale: the transient benefit remains visible near some task changes, but it is eventually outweighed by the stationary error of fitting too many past tasks jointly.

The supervised trajectories in Figure 26 make the same decomposition visible on real benchmarks. CLEAR is the closest empirical analogue of low drift: adjacent tasks share visual and semantic structure, so JTL can reuse previous training. MD5 and the synthetic CIFAR variants create sharper input or label shifts, making the historical data less aligned with the current task and increasing the Instability term.

Figure 27 highlights the contrast most directly. On CLEAR, the JTL trajectory tends to remain competitive or ahead because the stream changes gradually. On MD5, where each task corresponds to a more abrupt semantic change, JTL is frequently pulled toward earlier classes and ITL benefits from specializing to the current task. This is the empirical counterpart of the sign change in TE as Instability grows.

The window-agent zooms in Figure 28 illustrate why a finite memory can outperform both extremes. Early in training, the window and JTL agents are similar because little history exists. Later, the finite window discards obsolete tasks, retaining the transient benefit of recent replay while reducing the bias from distant tasks. This behavior matches the theoretical picture: the best history length grows when tasks are short or drift is slow, and shrinks when old tasks become poor predictors of the current optimum.
